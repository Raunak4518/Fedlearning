## Abstract

GeFL-F lets clients with different model architectures learn from each other through a shared frozen feature
extractor and a conditional feature generator trained by federated averaging. Its evaluation used balanced clients
only. We study it under long-tailed clients and locate where rare classes are lost, in three stages.

*The generator collapses rare classes.* With Adam's coupled weight decay and flat averaging, every client that lacks a
class shrinks that class's conditioning row by a step of Adam's size, whatever the decay coefficient. Tail rows held by
7–8 of 10 clients end at 4–34% of the head rows' length (10–100 clients). Removing the coupled decay (decoupled
AdamW, or no decay on the rows) restores them and recovers 2–11 points of balanced accuracy (7–11 with 10 clients). Averaging each row only over the clients that
hold its class adds a further 1.3–1.8 points at 50–100 clients on MNIST (same direction, not significant, on
FashionMNIST): the decay fix removes the collapse but leaves the dilution of rare classes.

*Most of the remaining loss is in the last layer.* A class-balanced last layer computed after training from
secure-aggregated class sums of each architecture's embeddings (exact readout, ER) lifts plain GeFL-F by 7–20 points
on five datasets. Any method for this setting must therefore be compared with a recalibrated GeFL-F.

*Exact statistics also improve the representation.* A generator anchored to exactly aggregated class statistics —
conditioned on the exact class mean, moment-corrected, with herding-style sample selection, and switching back to
class rows where class means carry no information — gives heads whose bodies support a better readout. With the same
ER readout on both methods, it beats GeFL-F by +3.9, +2.2, +2.0, +2.6 and +3.4 points on MNIST, FashionMNIST, SVHN,
CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100-LT (IF = 100, Dirichlet 0.5, 10 clients, seeds 0–2; every 95% confidence interval excludes
zero), and CCVR-style readouts give similar margins (+2.1 to +3.6). On balanced data it does no harm (within 0.6 points
once both have a readout). Recomputing the readout during training instead of once afterwards does not help (pilot).

On new seeds, with every method's settings and readout chosen on validation data, ours stays ahead of every baseline
on all five datasets, but by 0.5–2.0 points over the strongest one (the decay-fixed GeFL-F or real-data heads with
their best readout; significant on three datasets). So most of GeFL-F's long-tail loss is fixed by removing coupled
decay and computing the last layer from exact statistics; the anchored generator adds a consistent but modest gain.
With 10 clients each architecture has a single client, the all-client readouts need each architecture's body
broadcast once, and the extractor is small and frozen (CIFAR-100 accuracy is 15–19% for every method). Differential
privacy (zCDP, per example, trusted aggregator) on the four added statistics costs 4.5–6.9 points at ε = 8; the generator's and heads' training are not covered.

## 1. Introduction

*Motivation.* Federated deployments rarely have one model architecture or balanced labels. Hospitals run different
networks on different hardware, and rare conditions appear at only a few sites. Generator-based model-heterogeneous
FL (GeFL, and its feature-level variant GeFL-F) handles the first problem: a shared generative model, trained by
federated averaging, carries knowledge between architectures that cannot be averaged. Its evaluation used IID
clients only.

*Question.* When a class is held by few clients, everything the generator and the heads learn about it passes through
federated averaging and through the clients' own skewed label distributions. Where exactly is a rare class lost —
in the generator, in each head's representation, or in its last layer — and what does each stage cost?

*Answer, in three stages* (all under a 100:1 long tail with Dirichlet(0.5) clients):
1. **Generator (§3, §5.4).** Adam's coupled weight decay, combined with flat averaging, shrinks rare classes'
   conditioning rows on every client that lacks them (Theorem 1, Lemma 2). Removing the coupled decay fixes the
   collapse (2–11 points); holder-weighted averaging (HWA) adds 1–2 points as the number of clients grows.
2. **Last layer (§5.3).** Most of GeFL-F's remaining loss is in each head's last layer, which every client learns on its
   own label distribution. An exact, class-balanced readout from secure-aggregated sums recovers 7–20 points.
3. **Representation (§4.5, §5.3, §5.6).** A generator whose class information comes from exactly aggregated statistics
   gives better bodies: +2.0 to +3.9 over GeFL-F when both use the same exact readout (seeds 0–2), and 0.5–2.0 over
   the strongest baseline on new seeds with validation-chosen settings.

*Contributions.*
1. **A diagnosis** of rare-class collapse in a FedAvg-trained conditional generator (Theorem 1, Lemma 2), confirmed by
   intervention: removing the decay or averaging over holders restores the rows, and the decay coefficient does not
   matter (K15, T01).
2. **A decomposition of the long-tail loss** into generator, last-layer and representation parts, using eight
   federated readouts (ER, Fed3R, FedNCM, CCVR, each with all clients or each architecture's own clients) on every
   trained model, five datasets including CIFAR-100-LT (K16).
3. **An exact-statistics generator** (mean anchor, moment correction, herding-style selection, an $F$-based switch to
   class rows) whose bodies beat GeFL-F's under the same readout. Its components have precedents (§6); the
   contribution is their use for a federated generator and the measured representation gain.
4. **A recipe** for practitioners: remove coupled decay (or average rows over holders), train the anchored generator,
   and compute the last layer from exact sums after training (§4.6).
5. **Evidence** with honest margins: recalibrated baselines, the optimiser fixes, calibration and worst-class metrics,
   negative results (§5.7, Appendix B), and a validation-selected re-test on new seeds against the decay fix and
   published long-tail FL losses (§5.6), whose pre-registered predictions partly failed.

## 2. Background: GeFL-F

There are $K$ clients. Client $k$ holds data $D_k$ and runs a head $h_{a(k)}$ of architecture $a(k)$ from a set of $G$
architectures. GeFL-F proceeds in three stages.

1. **Feature extractor (FE) warm-up.** A small shared FE $\phi$ (one conv block; output $3\times16\times16$) is trained
   with the heads by FedAvg, then frozen.
2. **Feature generator.** A conditional VAE $G_F(h\mid y)$ (CVAE-F) is trained on $\phi(x)$ by FedAvg. It uses Adam
   with learning rate $10^{-3}$ and *coupled* weight decay $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ (paper, Table XV). The label enters
   through a learned linear projection whose $c$-th column (the *conditioning row* $w_c$) is the only class-specific
   parameter.
3. **Heads.** Each round, each client trains its head for $T_s = 1$ epoch on generated features with uniform random
   labels. It then trains for $T_r = 5$ epochs on its real features. Heads of the same architecture are averaged.

The paper reports `best_mean_acc`: the best test accuracy of each architecture over rounds, averaged over the
architectures.

## 3. Why rare classes vanish

### 3.1 The conditioning-row contraction bound

**Setting.** Class $c$'s conditioning row $r_c \in \mathbb R^{d}$ ($d = 512$) is the only class-specific parameter of
CVAE-F, and its data gradient is non-zero only on samples labelled $c$. In one round, client $k$ starts from the global
row $r_c$, runs $S_k$ local Adam steps with coupled weight decay $\lambda$, and returns $r_{k,c}$. Let $H_c$ be the
$m_c$ clients that hold class $c$ and $p_c = m_c/K$. Write each client's change in norm as
$\Delta_k = \|r_{k,c}\| - \|r_c\|$, the holders' mean gain as $\bar G_c = \frac{1}{m_c}\sum_{k\in H_c}\Delta_k$ and the
non-holders' mean loss as $\bar D_c = -\frac{1}{K-m_c}\sum_{k\notin H_c}\Delta_k$.

**Theorem 1 (contraction bound).** Under flat FedAvg, $r_c^+ = \frac1K\sum_k r_{k,c}$,
$$\|r_c^+\| \;\le\; \|r_c\| + p_c\,\bar G_c - (1-p_c)\,\bar D_c ,$$
with equality iff all $r_{k,c}$ point in the same direction. Hence the row contracts whenever
$$p_c\,\bar G_c < (1-p_c)\,\bar D_c \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \frac{m_c}{K} < \theta_c := \frac{\bar D_c}{\bar G_c + \bar D_c}
\qquad (\bar G_c + \bar D_c > 0),$$
and for every $m_c < K$ when $\bar G_c \le 0 < \bar D_c$. (For $m_c = K$ the bound still allows contraction when
holder updates point in different directions; T01 shows this in a few rounds for classes held by all clients.)

*Proof.* $\|\frac1K\sum_k r_{k,c}\| \le \frac1K\sum_k\|r_{k,c}\| = \|r_c\| + \frac1K\big(\sum_{k\in H_c}\Delta_k + \sum_{k\notin
H_c}\Delta_k\big)$, and the two sums are $m_c\bar G_c$ and $-(K-m_c)\bar D_c$. $\square$

The bound uses no property of the optimiser; it is the triangle inequality applied to the measured changes. Because
$\bar G_c$ and $\bar D_c$ are measured in the same round, $\theta_c$ is an ex-post description, not a prediction:
holders regrow a row that arrived shrunk, so $\bar G_c$ itself depends on how many non-holders there are. What the
optimiser decides is the size of $\bar D_c$ and $\bar G_c$.

**Lemma 2 (coupled decay under Adam).** A non-holder's gradient on $r_c$ is exactly $\lambda r_c$ (the same holds for a
holder on steps whose batch lacks $c$). Adam's update is invariant to a positive rescaling of the gradient sequence
up to $\epsilon$: $\hat m$ and $\sqrt{\hat v}$ both scale with $\lambda$. So as long as $\lambda |r_{c,j}| \gg \epsilon$,
a non-holder moves each coordinate of $r_c$ toward zero by a step of order the learning rate $\eta$ whatever the value
of $\lambda$, and $\bar D_c$ does not depend on $\lambda$ in that range. (Each client keeps its Adam state across rounds
while its parameters are reset to the global ones, so a single step can exceed $\eta$; numerically the invariance
holds for $\lambda \gtrsim 10^{-5}$ at these row norms and breaks down near $\lambda \approx 10^{-7}$.) At $\lambda = 0$ the gradient and both moments are zero and $\bar D_c = 0$
exactly. (Under SGD the same decay would cost only $\approx S\eta\lambda\|r_c\|$, $10^3$ times less at
$\eta = \lambda = 10^{-3}$.)

**What this means.** An earlier version of this result, "rows of classes held by fewer than half the clients
shrink", was wrong: it is the special case $\bar G_c = \bar D_c$, and its per-step bound had the wrong units (a norm
can move by up to $\sqrt d\,\eta$ per Adam step). Every holder would have to grow the row as fast as a non-holder
shrinks it. Nothing forces that. A holder's data gradient fits the row; it does not push its norm up at Adam's full
rate. So $\bar G_c \ll \bar D_c$, $\theta_c > 1/2$, and **tail rows contract even when most clients hold the class**.
Without weight decay the non-holder term vanishes; the data step is then only diluted by $m_c/K$ (measured in
`experiments/results/T02_dilution_rates/summary.md`): rare classes learn more slowly but do not collapse.

**Predictions.** (i) GeFL-F's tail-to-head row-norm ratio is $\ll 1$ under a long tail, even when every class is held
by more than half the clients. (ii) It is the same for every $\lambda$ with $\lambda\|r\| \gg \epsilon$ and disappears
at $\lambda = 0$. (iii) It worsens with $K$ for fixed data, because more clients are non-holders of each tail class.
(iv) Removing non-holders from row $c$'s average (HWA) removes the $\bar D_c$ term; the row can still shrink when
holder updates cancel, but no longer systematically.

### 3.2 Verification on the real generator (T01)

We instrumented the paper's CVAE-F and logged every client's row norms after every local round (MNIST, IF = 100,
Dir 0.5, $K = 10$, 40 generator rounds, seed 0; `experiments/T01_collapse_threshold.py`, figure F01_collapse).

* *Consistency check.* Theorem 1 is satisfied in all 1,200 row-rounds of the three flat-averaging arms (mean
  $\|r_c^+\|$/bound $= 0.983$). Since the bound is computed from the same measured norms, this only confirms the
  instrumentation; the evidence for the mechanism is the next three points.
* *$K/2$ is the wrong threshold.* Every class is held by at least 7 of the 10 clients, so the half-the-clients rule
  predicts no contraction anywhere. Yet the rows of classes 7–9 shrank to 4–10% of their initial norm and those of
  classes 5–6 to 65–75%, while head rows (held by all 10 clients) grew by a factor 1.4. Holders gained only
  $\bar G_c/\bar D_c = 0.07$–$0.64$ (median per class) of what non-holders lost, so $\theta_c = 0.61$–$0.93$.
  Theorem 1 certifies 139 of the 197 contractions; the rest come from holder updates that partly cancel in
  direction, which the norm bound does not use.
* *Only the presence of decay matters.* $\lambda = 10^{-4}$ and $\lambda = 10^{-3}$ give the same trajectories (final
  tail/head ratio 0.155 vs 0.152; non-holder loss $1.49\times10^{-2}$ per step in both). At $\lambda = 0$ the ratio
  stays at 0.85 and only 2 of 400 row-rounds contract.
* *HWA removes the systematic contraction.* With the same decay ($\lambda = 10^{-3}$), aggregating the rows over holders only
  makes the tail rows *grow* to 1.17–1.25× their initial norm (against 0.04–0.10 under flat averaging); they shrink
  in 1–6 of 40 rounds instead of 35–40. The final tail/head ratio is 0.72, against 0.15, because head rows grow
  faster still.

**Other generators.** The paper's DCGAN-F and DDPM-F are trained without weight decay. Then $\bar D_c = 0$
exactly (a non-holder's gradient on class $c$'s parameters is zero; checked on T01 at $\lambda = 0$: largest
non-holder change 0.0 in 40 rounds), and flat averaging gives $r_c^+ - r_c = p_c \times$ (mean holder update): dilution,
not contraction. Rare classes still learn slowly, mainly because a holder moves the row only on steps whose batch
contains the class; the predicted per-class rate $\frac1K\sum_k S_k\,[1-(1-\pi_{k,c})^B]$ matches the measured row
growth on T01 (Spearman 0.85, $p = 0.002$; `experiments/T02_dilution_rates.py`). HWA speeds the rarest rows up by
about 1× at $K = 10$ and 2–3× at $K = 100$. The shared parameters see the rarest class in 0.4% of the loss, the same
imbalance under which centralised diffusion models and conditional GANs already lose their tail classes [CBDM,
CVPR 2023; gSR, ECCV 2022], and class conditioning alone can cause mode collapse in limited-data GANs [Shahbazi et
al., "Collapse by Conditioning", ICLR 2022]. The new element here is the federated optimiser–aggregation mechanism,
not class-conditional collapse as such. The size of the resulting accuracy loss for DCGAN-F / DDPM-F under the long tail is not
yet measured.

The setting is IF = 100 with Dirichlet(0.5), $K = 10$, full schedule and 3 seeds (F01). *Fidelity* is the accuracy of

|---|---|---|---|---|

| + HWA + LA | 0.80 (0.71, 0.80, 0.88) | 27.2 | 1.01 (1.15, 1.03, 0.85) | 66.2 |

On SVHN (Kaggle, 3 seeds) the GeFL-F ratio is 0.49, 0.32 and 0.18, against 0.99, 0.96 and 1.05 with HWA. A quick-pass

### 3.4 The intervention at full scale: fixing the optimiser vs fixing the aggregation (K15)

If coupled decay on non-holders causes the collapse, any fix that removes it should restore the rows. K15 tests the
existing fixes against HWA, all with logit adjustment (LA) in the heads, on the same splits and seeds as the
references (long tail, 3 seeds; tables T17 / T18):

| balanced accuracy | MNIST K=10 | K=50 | K=100 | FMNIST K=10 | K=50 | K=100 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| GeFL-F + LA (coupled decay, as in the paper) | 79.2 | 81.4 | 79.5 | 65.2 | 66.5 | 66.6 |
| + AdamW, decay 1e-3 (decoupled) | 86.4 | 84.7 | 81.6 | 75.9 | 74.0 | 71.8 |
| + no decay on the class rows only | 86.8 | 84.4 | 81.8 | 75.9 | 74.5 | 71.9 |
| + lazy decay (only rows in the batch) | 85.1 | 83.9 | 81.7 | 75.8 | 74.2 | 71.6 |
| + HWA (coupled decay kept) | **87.9** | **86.5** | **83.1** | 76.0 | **75.5** | **73.3** |
| HWA minus best fix [95% CI] | +1.1 [−0.9, 3.1] | +1.8 [0.3, 3.2] | +1.3 [0.6, 2.0] | −0.1 | +0.9 (n.s.) | +1.4 (n.s.) |

* **The diagnosis holds.** Every fix restores the tail rows (tail/head norm 0.73–0.97, against 0.04–0.34 under coupled
  decay) and recovers 2–11 points over GeFL-F + LA (7–11 with 10 clients, less as the number of clients grows). AdamW at decay 1e-2 equals AdamW at 1e-3 (86.5 vs 86.4 on MNIST, 76.1 vs 75.9 on
  FashionMNIST), and the row-only fix equals the global one: the decay *term on the rows* is the cause.
* **HWA's extra gain is the dilution term.** Without collapse, a rare row still learns at a rate diluted by $m_c/K$
  (§3.2). HWA removes that too. Its advantage over the best optimiser fix is 1.3–1.8 points at K = 50 / 100 on MNIST
  (confidence intervals exclude zero) and points the same way on FashionMNIST; tail recall shows it more clearly
  (MNIST, K = 50: 74.6 vs 69.9).
* **Practical reading.** Removing coupled decay is the first fix and costs nothing (2–11 points); HWA is a refinement worth 1–2
  points that grows with the number of clients. The anchored generator of §4.5 has no class rows at all, so for it the
  question does not arise; HWA matters for the minimal variant and for the class-row branch used on SVHN.

## 4. Method

### 4.1 HWA: holder-weighted aggregation of conditioning rows

Let $n_{k,c}$ be client $k$'s count of class $c$, and $E(n) = (1-\beta^n)/(1-\beta)$ the effective number of samples
(Cui et al., 2019), with $\beta = 0.999$. The server aggregates

$$w_c \leftarrow \frac{\sum_k E(n_{k,c})\, w_c^{(k)}}{\sum_k E(n_{k,c})}, \qquad\text{all other parameters by FedAvg.}$$

* **Effect on §3.1.** Non-holders have $E(0) = 0$, so the decay-only copies are excluded. By the same triangle
  inequality, $\|r_c^+\| \le \|r_c\| + \sum_{k\in H_c}\omega_k\Delta_k$ with $\omega_k \propto E(n_{k,c})$: the
  $-(1-p_c)\bar D_c$ term of Theorem 1 disappears for every class with at least one holder. HWA does not force the row
  to grow; it leaves it to the holders, weighted toward those with the most samples.
* **Why effective number (a heuristic).** A holder's row estimate has variance that falls with its sample count but
  plausibly saturates (correlated samples, a finite number of local steps). $E(n)$-weighting is a convenient
  interpolation between the two limits, raw counts $n$ ($\beta \to 1$) and uniform-over-holders ($\beta \to 0$); we do
  not derive it. K02 ablates both limits: the three rules are within one point of each other (counts 89.97 / 76.45,
  uniform 89.02 / 76.33, $E(n)$ 90.15 / 76.51 on MNIST / FMNIST; 2 seeds), so what matters is excluding non-holders,
  not the exact weights.
* **Do-no-harm.** When every client has the same class counts, HWA *equals* FedAvg. Random IID splits give nearly
  equal counts. On SVHN IID, HWA + LA scores 0.18 points below GeFL-F (75.64 vs 75.82), a small but consistent cost.
* **Privacy.** The server needs $\sum_k E(n_{k,c}) w_c^{(k)}$ and $\sum_k E(n_{k,c})$, which are sums. Under secure
  aggregation it learns neither any client's row nor any client's histogram. We also test Laplace-noised counts
  ($\varepsilon \in \{10, 1, 0.1\}$; K02).

### 4.2 LA: heads trained with the client's own prior

Each head trains on real features with logits $f(h) + \tau \log \pi_k$, where $\pi_k$ is client $k$'s smoothed label
distribution and $\tau = 1$. Testing uses plain $f(h)$. Cross-entropy on prior-adjusted logits is Fisher-consistent
for the balanced error (Menon et al., 2021). Using the *client's own* prior means each local update estimates the
balanced risk for that client's label shift, so averaged heads are balanced as well. The prior never leaves the client.
In IID data $\pi_k$ is uniform and LA is a no-op.

Why it is needed in addition to HWA: the real phase ($T_r = 5$ epochs) follows the synthetic phase ($T_s = 1$) every
round, and gradient descent contracts toward the real-risk optimum. The final head therefore reflects the client's
skew unless the real phase itself is debiased.

### 4.3 CSL: consensus soft labels for synthetic features

A generated feature $\tilde h \sim G_F(\cdot\mid y)$ is not always a clean example of $y$. Its true posterior
$p(\cdot\mid\tilde h)$ differs from $\mathrm{onehot}(y)$ by the generator's error. The minimiser of expected
cross-entropy is that posterior, so one-hot training on generated features injects label noise. The server already
holds every architecture's aggregated head. For a shared synthetic pool drawn from a public seed it computes

$$t(\tilde h) = (1-\beta)\,\mathrm{onehot}(y) + \beta\,\frac1G\sum_{g=1}^{G}\mathrm{softmax}\,h_g(\tilde h), \qquad \beta = 0.5,$$

and clients use $t$ as the synthetic-phase target. The $G$ heads have different architectures and are trained on
different clients' data. Averaging their errors lowers variance by up to $1/G$ for uncorrelated errors, and the one-hot
term bounds the bias that a shared ensemble error would introduce. This is the bias–variance reason why $\beta = 0.5$
beats $\beta = 1$ (§B.4). The bias–variance view of distillation from teachers that
approximate the Bayes posterior is due to Menon et al. (ICML 2021); CSL applies it to generated features labelled by a
cross-architecture ensemble.

*Protocol and cost.* GeFL-F aggregates heads only within an architecture, so the server must evaluate one head per
architecture on the pool. In our implementation it holds the per-architecture aggregated heads (as the server of a
model-heterogeneous FedAvg does when it aggregates them); a deployment that does not keep heads at the server needs each
architecture group to upload its predictions on the pool (a $P \times C$ sum per group per round, computable under
secure aggregation). CSL therefore needs a message GeFL-F does not: head parameters or pool predictions per round. The
server broadcasts the pool seed and the $P \times C$ soft labels (about 24 KB per round here). CSL uses no real data.

### 4.4 Why consensus labels make more synthetic data useful (Proposition 3)

**Stylised model.** A head estimates a parameter $\theta^\star$ from $n_r$ real samples and a random fraction
$\lambda \in [0, 1]$ of $n_s$ synthetic samples, each at unit weight (a subsampling model; more synthetic epochs raise
the number of synthetic samples used). This is a toy model of the head's training, not of SGD on the actual network. Real samples are unbiased with per-sample variance $\sigma^2$.
Synthetic samples carry the same variance, but their labels are biased by $b(t)$, the distance between the label
target $t$ and the true posterior of the generated feature. The weighted estimator's mean-squared error is

$$\mathcal{E}(\lambda) \;=\; \frac{\sigma^2}{n_r + \lambda n_s} \;+\; \Big(\frac{\lambda n_s}{n_r+\lambda n_s}\Big)^{2} b^2 .$$

**Proposition 3.** For $b > 0$, $\mathcal{E}$ has a unique minimiser $\lambda^\star(b)$ in $[0,1]$ (or the boundary
$\lambda^\star = 1$). It is non-increasing in $b$, and $\lambda^\star \to 1$ as $b \to 0$.

*Proof.* Write $w = \lambda n_s/(n_r+\lambda n_s) \in [0, w_{\max}]$, with $w_{\max} = n_s/(n_r+n_s)$. This is an
increasing bijection of $\lambda$. Since $n_r + \lambda n_s = n_r/(1-w)$, the error becomes

$$\mathcal{E}(w) = \frac{\sigma^2 (1-w)}{n_r} + w^2 b^2 .$$

This is a convex parabola in $w$, minimised at $w^\star = \sigma^2/(2 n_r b^2)$ and clipped to $[0, w_{\max}]$.
$w^\star$ is decreasing in $b$, and $\lambda$ is increasing in $w$. So $\lambda^\star$ is non-increasing in $b$. As
$b \to 0$, $w^\star \to \infty$, so the clip gives $w^\star = w_{\max}$, that is $\lambda^\star = 1$. $\square$

**Reading.** With hard labels, $b$ is the generator's label error, which no amount of synthetic data averages away.
The optimal synthetic share is then small, and adding synthetic epochs ($T_s\!\uparrow$, which acts like raising
$\lambda n_s$) past it *hurts*, or at best does nothing. This is the paper's Fig. 10. CSL's target
$t = (1-\beta)e_y + \beta\,\bar p$ moves $t$ toward the posterior, which lowers $b$ and pushes $\lambda^\star$ up, so the
same extra epochs now help. The proposition predicts an *interaction*, not a main effect: the gain from more synthetic
epochs should be larger with CSL than without it. That is exactly what E16 measures (FMNIST IID: +0.22 from $T_s = 1$
to 5 without CSL, +0.83 with it). It also explains why the budget helps more under the long tail once HWA has made
tail features faithful, since a lower generator error is a lower $b$ as well.

### 4.5 Anchoring every stage to exact statistics

HWA repairs a FedAvg-trained generator. The diagnosis of §3, together with the measurements of §5, points to a
cleaner design. Wherever the data decides the result, use a statistic that secure aggregation computes **exactly** —
sums over clients of per-class quantities — instead of a parameter that FedAvg *estimates*. Every statistic below
costs one upload per client and reveals only federation-level sums.

**PC-VAE: class identity from the exact class mean.**
* *Prototypes.* The server computes $m_c = \sum_k \sum_{i \in k, y_i = c} h_i \,/\, \sum_k n_{kc}$.
* *Decoder.* It models only the within-class residual, $\tilde h = \mathrm{ReLU}(m_y + \mathrm{dec}(z, m_y))$, and
  receives $m_y$ through a class-*shared* projection.
* *Encoder.* It sees $m_y$ as an extra channel.
* *Consequence.* There is no class-specific parameter, so the collapse recursion of §3 has nothing to act on.
  Tail classes borrow within-class variation from every class through the shared decoder. A linear decoder recovers
  a Gaussian class-conditional model. The anchor does **not** guarantee that samples carry their class: the decoder
  can ignore $m_y$, and when class means are close it does (next paragraph, and SVHN in §B.11).

**What a mean-anchored generator can express.** The anchor removes the collapse, but it also limits what the
generator can say about a class. Write the class-$c$ sampler as $X_c = \mathrm{ReLU}(m_c + g(Z, A m_c))$, with
$Z \sim \mathcal N(0, I)$ the same for every class, $A$ the class-shared projection, and $g$ $L$-Lipschitz in its
second argument.

*Proposition 5.* For any two classes, $W_1(G_c, G_{c'}) \le (1 + L\|A\|_2)\,\|m_c - m_{c'}\|$.

*Proof.* Couple both classes through the same $Z$. Since ReLU is 1-Lipschitz,
$\|X_c - X_{c'}\| \le \|m_c - m_{c'}\| + \|g(Z, A m_c) - g(Z, A m_{c'})\| \le (1 + L\|A\|_2)\|m_c - m_{c'}\|$.
Taking expectations bounds $W_1$. $\square$

* *What it means for a head.* If a head's logit differences are $K$-Lipschitz, the synthetic data can separate $c$
  from $c'$ by an expected margin of at most $K(1 + L\|A\|_2)\|m_c - m_{c'}\|$, however different the real classes
  are. MC adds at most the difference of the exact spreads, and KH only re-weights candidates drawn from $G_c$.
* *The quantity that decides.* In units of the within-class spread the anchors are, on average, $\sqrt{2F}$ apart,
  where $F = \overline{\|m_c - \bar m\|^2}\,/\,\overline{V_c}$. The server computes $F$ from the sums it already
  receives. In the frozen feature space where the gate computes it (logged as `class_sep_F` in every run), $F$ is
  0.40–0.71 (FashionMNIST), 0.25–0.36 (MNIST), 0.055–0.078 (CIFAR-10) and **0.005–0.013 (SVHN)**, the range being
  over splits and settings.
* *Why training does not escape it.* On SVHN the anchors differ by about 10–15% of the within-class spread
  ($\sqrt{2F}$ with $F \approx 0.005$–$0.013$). Reproducing class differences of the order of the spread needs a decoder
  roughly 6–10 times more sensitive to the anchor than to its noise. The ELBO offers a cheaper route: the encoder sees $x$, so it can carry the label
  in $z$ for a KL cost of order $\log C$ nats per sample, small against the reconstruction term. Samples drawn from
  the prior are then class-agnostic. This is what we measure (§B.11): after KH, tail fidelity is 24% on SVHN, against
  36% for the CVAE + HWA and 0.9 on MNIST.
* *The fix.* Free class rows $r_y$ inside the decoder, $g(Z, A m_y + r_y)$, remove the bound: the separation
  $\|r_c - r_{c'}\|$ is then a learned parameter, and HWA keeps it from collapsing. This is the hybrid generator PCR.

**MC: the minimum-displacement moment correction.** The server also knows each class's exact spread
$V_c = \mathbb E\|h - m_c\|^2$, from the sums of $\|h\|^2$. Let $\tilde m_c$ and $\tilde V_c$ be the mean and spread
of the generator's class-$c$ samples. MC maps $x \mapsto \mathrm{ReLU}\!\big(m_c + \sqrt{V_c/\tilde V_c}\,(x - \tilde m_c)\big)$.

*Proposition 4.* Among **all** maps $T$ with $\mathbb E\,T(X) = m_c$ and $\mathbb E\|T(X) - m_c\|^2 = V_c$ (with
$X$ the generator's class-$c$ sample), the map $T^\star(x) = m_c + \sqrt{V_c/\tilde V_c}\,(x - \tilde m_c)$ minimises
the expected displacement $\mathbb E\|T(X) - X\|^2$.

*Proof.* Let $Y = T(X)$. Since $\mathbb E[Y - m_c] = \mathbb E[X - \tilde m_c] = 0$,
$\mathbb E\|Y - X\|^2 = V_c + \tilde V_c + \|m_c - \tilde m_c\|^2 - 2\,\mathbb E\langle Y - m_c,\, X - \tilde m_c\rangle$.
By Cauchy–Schwarz, the last expectation is at most $\sqrt{V_c\tilde V_c}$, with equality iff
$Y - m_c = \sqrt{V_c/\tilde V_c}\,(X - \tilde m_c)$. $\square$

In the idealised setting of Proposition 4, MC matches the two moments the generator is measurably wrong about while
changing the samples as little as possible. In practice two things break exactness: the final ReLU (which projects onto
the non-negative orthant where FE features live) changes both moments again, and $\tilde m_c, \tilde V_c$ are plug-in
estimates from a finite batch of generated samples. MC is therefore an approximate moment match.

**MC-S: the same correction with the exact class covariance.** Second moments are also sums.
* *Release.* The pooled within-class covariance (the release FSG already uses) defines a top-$k$ principal basis
  $B$, which the server broadcasts. Clients then upload class sums of $z = B^\top h$ and of $z z^\top$
  ($C k^2$ numbers; $k = 256$).
* *Target.* For each class this gives the exact class covariance $\Sigma_c$ in that subspace. For tail classes it is
  shrunk toward the pooled covariance with prior strength $k$ (an inverse-Wishart prior), and rescaled to keep the
  class's exact in-subspace variance. Only the *shape* is borrowed.
* *Map.* Let $\tilde\Sigma_c$ be the generator's class-$c$ covariance in the subspace. MC-S maps
  $x \mapsto \mathrm{ReLU}\big(m_c + B A_c B^\top (x - \tilde m_c) + s_c (I - BB^\top)(x - \tilde m_c)\big)$, with
  $A_c = \tilde\Sigma_c^{-1/2}\big(\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\Sigma_c\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}\tilde\Sigma_c^{-1/2}$.
  The scalar $s_c$ restores the exact spread outside the subspace, as in MC.
* *Practical details.* $\tilde\Sigma_c$ is estimated from generated samples and regularised (shrinkage toward its
  diagonal and eigenvalue clipping) before the matrix square roots; the map is applied before the ReLU, so the same
  caveats as for MC apply. The release adds a $D \times D$ pooled covariance (5.0–28.8 MB depending on the feature
  dimension), which matters for the cost comparison in §B.12.

*Proposition 6.* Among all maps $T$ with $\mathbb E\,T(X) = m_c$ and $\mathrm{Cov}\,T(X) = \Sigma_c$ (with $X$ the
generator's class-$c$ sample, $\tilde\Sigma_c \succ 0$), the map $T^\star(x) = m_c + A_c(x - \tilde m_c)$ minimises
$\mathbb E\|T(X) - X\|^2$. The minimum is the squared Gelbrich distance
$\|m_c - \tilde m_c\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma_c + \mathrm{tr}\,\tilde\Sigma_c - 2\,\mathrm{tr}\big(\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\Sigma_c\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}$.

*Proof.* Let $Y = T(X)$ and $K = \mathbb E[(Y - m_c)(X - \tilde m_c)^\top]$. Then
$\mathbb E\|Y - X\|^2 = \|m_c - \tilde m_c\|^2 + \mathrm{tr}\,\Sigma_c + \mathrm{tr}\,\tilde\Sigma_c - 2\,\mathrm{tr}\,K$.
The joint covariance $\left[\begin{smallmatrix}\Sigma_c & K\\ K^\top & \tilde\Sigma_c\end{smallmatrix}\right]$ is
positive semi-definite, so $K = \Sigma_c^{1/2} R\, \tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}$ with $\|R\|_2 \le 1$. Hence
$\mathrm{tr}\,K \le \|\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\Sigma_c^{1/2}\|_* = \mathrm{tr}\big(\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\Sigma_c\tilde\Sigma_c^{1/2}\big)^{1/2}$.
$T^\star$ attains the bound. It gives $K = A_c\tilde\Sigma_c$, whose trace is exactly this value, and
$\mathrm{Cov}\,T^\star(X) = A_c\tilde\Sigma_cA_c = \Sigma_c$. $\square$

In the subspace-plus-complement form, the displacement splits orthogonally. The in-subspace part is bounded by
Proposition 6 and the complement part by Proposition 4, and the MC-S map attains both bounds at once. MC-S is
therefore the minimum-displacement map that achieves the exact class mean, the exact class covariance in the
principal subspace and the exact spread outside it. The bound itself is classical (Gelbrich, 1990; Olkin and
Pukelsheim, 1982). What is new is using exact federated second moments to correct a federated generator.

*Why it matters.* By Proposition 5, a mean-anchored generator cannot tell classes apart when their means coincide.
MC-S gives each class its exact covariance, so it can, wherever the covariances differ.
* *Information in exact statistics* (pixel space, balanced accuracy, classifiers built only from the statistics):

  | | nearest class mean | LDA (FSG's model) | QDA (class covariances) |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | MNIST | 80.4 | **87.2** | 79.8 |
  | FashionMNIST | 67.8 | **81.3** | 74.7 |
  | CIFAR-10 | 28.2 | 39.3 | **51.4** |
  | SVHN | 10.3 | 20.6 | **54.4** |

* *Proxy test.* A class-agnostic generator (the SVHN failure mode, simulated by drawing from the pooled data) is
  corrected per class. A head is trained only on the corrected samples and tested on real data:

  | balanced / long tail | MC (mean + spread) | **MC-S (mean + covariance)** | real data, same counts |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | MNIST | 65.2 / 63.4 | **89.7 / 78.1** | 95.1 / 87.1 |
  | CIFAR-10 | 22.6 / 23.0 | **35.2 / 30.5** | 43.7 / 33.7 |
  | SVHN | 10.6 / 12.2 | **43.9 / 28.6** | 69.6 / 51.9 |

  MC transfers nothing on SVHN. MC-S transfers 63% of real-data accuracy in the balanced case and 55% under the
  long tail. The full-pipeline test is K11 (§B.11).

**KH: kernel herding toward the exact class embedding.**
* *Embedding.* With bounded random Fourier features $\phi$, the server receives the exact class mean embeddings
  $\mu_c = \sum_k \sum_{i \in k, y_i = c}\phi(h_i) / n_c$ in one upload.
* *Selection.* For each class, from $4n$ calibrated candidates, KH greedily selects $n$ without replacement so that
  $\|\frac1n\sum_t \phi(x_t) - \mu_c\|$ stays small (a herding-style greedy selection; it is not the Frank–Wolfe
  kernel-herding iteration, which samples from the whole space and may repeat points).
* *Motivation, not a guarantee.* For a head whose loss lies in the RKHS with norm at most $B$,
  $|\mathbb E_{G_c}\ell - \mathbb E_{P_c}\ell| \le B\,\mathrm{MMD}(G_c, P_c)$. The premise does not hold for the
  ReLU heads we train, so this only motivates the target. Empirically KH lowers the MMD but does not improve
  train-on-synthetic, test-on-real accuracy, and its effect on the final accuracy is small and not significant
  (§B.11).

**BBC: balanced bias calibration on the anchored generator.**
* *Fit.* After training, the server fits $C$ logit offsets $b_g$ per head $g$ so that the head predicts every class
  equally often on class-balanced synthetic data. This is the prior-correction fixed point
  $b \leftarrow b - \log(C\,\bar q(b))$ of Saerens et al. (2002) in logit form.
* *Gate.* It is switched on only when the exact global class counts are imbalanced (max/min > 2). In our settings the
  ratio is near 1 (IID) or near the imbalance factor (long tail), so other thresholds in a wide range would make the
  same decisions, but no other threshold was run. It uses no test or held-out data.
* *Role.* It corrects the residual bias that LA's prior correction leaves when tail classes are also intrinsically
  hard (FashionMNIST's shirt and coat), without tuning LA's temperature.

**VCT: vicinal consensus transfer (heads).** The federation already knows more than any of its heads. On CIFAR-10
the ten heterogeneous heads *together* reach 69.8% while the average head reaches 60%. Consensus labels on synthetic
features (CSL) transfer little of that gap (+0.75), because synthetic features sit off the real data. Image-space
augmentation helps for a different reason, regularising each model on its own data (GeFL + MixUp: 62.67).
* *Rule.* In its real-data epochs, client $k$ mixes every real feature with a partner from the round's synthetic
  pool: $\tilde x = \lambda x + (1 - \lambda) x_s$, with $\lambda = \max(u, 1 - u)$ and $u \sim \mathrm{Beta}(1, 1)$.
* *Target.* $\lambda\,e_y + (1 - \lambda)\,q(x_s)$, where $q(x_s)$ is the partner's consensus soft label from all
  architectures.
* *Long tail.* LA uses the mixed sample's own label distribution, $\lambda\,\pi_k + (1 - \lambda)\,u_C$.
* *Disclosure.* Nothing new is disclosed. The pool and its consensus labels are the CSL release, and the real
  features never leave the client.

*Proposition 7 (MixUp is determined by class moments to second order).* Let the cross-entropy MixUp objective use
partners $(x', y') \sim Q$. Expand it to second order in $\delta = (1 - \lambda)(x' - x)$. It then depends on $Q$
only through the class prior $\pi_c$, the class means $\mu_c$ and the class covariances $\Sigma_c$.

*Proof.* Cross-entropy is linear in the target, so the objective is
$\mathbb E[\lambda\,\ell(f(\tilde x), y) + (1 - \lambda)\sum_c y'_c\,\ell(f(\tilde x), e_c)]$. Expand
$\ell(f(x + \delta), t) = \ell(f(x), t) + \nabla_t^\top\delta + \tfrac12\delta^\top H_t\delta + O(\|\delta\|^3)$. Given
$\lambda$, the expectation over $Q$ needs $\mathbb E[y'_c] = \pi_c$,
$\mathbb E[y'_c\,\delta] = (1 - \lambda)\,\pi_c(\mu_c - x)$ and
$\mathbb E[y'_c\,\delta\delta^\top] = (1 - \lambda)^2\,\pi_c\,[\Sigma_c + (\mu_c - x)(\mu_c - x)^\top]$. The first term's
moments are the $\pi$-mixtures of these. $\square$

* *Numerical check.* With skewed, non-Gaussian classes, the MixUp loss with real partners and with Gaussian partners
  that match only $(\pi_c, \mu_c, \Sigma_c)$ agree to within 0.0003–0.0010 (losses near 2.2). Partners that match only
  the means are off by up to 0.069.
* *Consequence.* Partners from the MC-S-corrected generator, which match the exact class means and covariances,
  give each client the MixUp regulariser of the *whole federation's* data. No client sees another's data. Under the
  long tail this matters most: a client cannot mix toward classes it does not hold, but the synthetic partners cover
  every class. The consensus part of the target is not moment-determined. It is the distillation of the ensemble along
  the path from the client's real data into the federation's knowledge.

**KME-Gen: the extreme case.** Train the generator *at the server only*, by MMD against the exact class embeddings
$\mu_c$, with no federated generator training at all. This is the DP-MERF construction (Harder et al., 2021) applied
to federated feature sums; we include it as a reference point, not as a contribution.
* *Partition invariance.* $\mu_c$ equals the centralised embedding for every partition, so collapse, dilution and
  client drift cannot occur.
* *Formal privacy.* Since $\|\phi\| \le \sqrt2$, Gaussian-mechanism DP on the single release covers the whole
  synthetic channel by post-processing.
* *Cost.* One upload of about 0.3 MB replaces 100 rounds of generator FedAvg.
* *Result.* It beats GeFL-F by 13–16 points, but a learned decoder anchored by PC + MC + KH is better (§B.11).

### 4.6 Exact readout (ER): the last layer from exact sums

After training, the server replaces each architecture's last layer by the class-balanced linear discriminant computed
from exact statistics of that architecture's embeddings $e = \mathrm{body}_g(h)$:
* every client passes its real features through the body of architecture $g$ and uploads per-class counts $n_c$,
  sums $\sum e$ and the sum $\sum e e^\top$ (secure-aggregatable);
* the server forms class means $\mu_c$ and the pooled within-class covariance $S$, shrinks it,
  $S_\gamma = (1-\gamma)S + \gamma\,\mathrm{tr}(S)/d\, I$ with $\gamma = 0.1$, and sets
  $w_c = S_\gamma^{-1}\mu_c$, $b_c = -\tfrac12 \mu_c^\top S_\gamma^{-1}\mu_c$ (uniform class prior: balanced error).

*Scope and cost.* With "all clients", every architecture's body (small: up to about $10^5$ parameters) is broadcast once
to every client, and each client uploads up to about $5\times10^5$ numbers per architecture ($d \le 1024$, symmetric
$\sum ee^\top$): one extra round. With "own clients" only the architecture's clients contribute, as in published
per-model protocols (CCVR, Fed3R, FedNCM); at K = 10 that is one client, and those readouts are much weaker for every
method (§5.3). ER is the LDA member of a family of closed-form readouts (CCVR, Fed3R, FedNCM); §5.3 reports all of them.

*Once, not during training.* Recomputing the readout every round and training only the bodies under it (ERL) was
tested and does not help (§5.7).

### 4.7 The method

**Ours = anchored generator + LA/CSL heads + exact readout.**
* *Generator.* $F = \overline{\|m_c - \bar m\|^2} / \overline{V_c}$ is computed from the released sums.
  $F \ge 0.05$: the PC-VAE, conditioned on the exact class mean (no class rows). $F < 0.05$: the paper's CVAE-F with
  class rows protected by HWA (or, equivalently for the collapse, by decoupled decay; §3.4). Implemented as the `GATE`
  component, read at run time. The threshold was set after the SVHN failure; measured $F$ is 0.005–0.013 on SVHN,
  0.054–0.080 on CIFAR-10, 0.18–0.25 on CIFAR-100 and at least 0.25 on MNIST / FashionMNIST, so CIFAR-10 lies close to
  it (its row branch is tested in K17).
* *Sampling.* MC (exact mean and spread), then KH (herding toward the exact class embedding).
* *Heads.* LA by the client's own prior, CSL on synthetic features; $T_s = 10$ on MNIST / FashionMNIST and 1 elsewhere
  (chosen on test results so far; K17 chooses it on validation data).
* *Readout.* ER after training (§4.6); BBC is an alternative that needs no extra round but is weaker.

**The minimal variant** for an existing GeFL-F deployment is HWA (or decoupled decay) + LA + CSL, with the paper's
generator, plus ER.

## 5. Experiments

### 5.1 Protocol

We follow the paper's appendix exactly.
* **Models.** FE per Table XIV (CIFAR-10: 10 channels). Heads CNN-1…10 (Tables XXI–XXIII). CVAE-F per Table XX.
* **Optimisation.** SGD with learning rate 0.1 and momentum 0. Rounds $T_{FE}/T_{KA}/T_{TN}$ = 20/100/50 (MNIST,
  FMNIST); SVHN and CIFAR-10 per the paper.
* **Data.** Fraction 0.1 (0.5 for CIFAR-10). Client-to-architecture assignment is contiguous. With $K = 10$ clients
  and 10 head architectures, **each architecture has exactly one client**, so "aggregating heads within an
  architecture" is a no-op at $K = 10$ and only matters at $K = 50, 100$.
* **Long tail.** $n_c \propto \mathrm{IF}^{c/(C-1)}$ with the total data budget fixed, and Dirichlet(0.5) client
  shares. Test sets are balanced. The rare classes are always classes 6–9 (the order is not permuted across seeds).
  With $K = 10$ the rarest classes are still held by 6–8 of the 10 clients. On CIFAR-10 the subsampled training set
  (18,485 images) has a realised imbalance of 48:1, not 100:1, because the rarest classes are capped by the fraction.
* **Metrics.** Final-round balanced accuracy is the primary metric. We also report tail recall (classes 6–9),
  worst-class recall, and the paper's `best_mean_acc`, which takes the best round of the test-set mean accuracy and is
  therefore optimistic; wherever we report it we give the final-round value beside it.
* **CIFAR-100-LT.** CIFAR-10's extractor and schedule, heads with 100 outputs, fraction 0.22: at IF = 100 the
  federation holds 10,926 images, 480 in the largest class and 5 in the smallest (the standard CIFAR-100-LT shape);
  head / medium / tail are the first 30 / next 30 / last 40% of classes.
* **Readouts.** Every trained model is scored as trained and under BBC, ER, Fed3R, FedNCM and CCVR (each with all
  clients or the architecture's own clients) and the pooled-real oracle (K16, K17).
* **Further metrics.** Macro-F1, G-mean of class recalls (each floored at 0.01), worst architecture, ECE, NLL, Brier
  score, and accuracy re-weighted to each head's own clients' label distribution.
* **Selection.** Until K17 there was no validation split: $T_s$, $\beta$, $\tau$ and the gate threshold were chosen on
  the test sets we report (§7). From K17 on, the 2,000 held-out training images that no client holds serve as a
  validation set, and settings are chosen on them only.
* **Statistics.** 3 seeds unless marked; each seed fixes the data split and the initialisation for every method,
  but GPU training is not bit-deterministic (cuDNN autotuning was on), so a repeated seed does not reproduce exactly.
  p-values come from two-sided paired t-tests over seeds, without multiplicity correction; with 2–3 seeds they are
  indicative only.

* **Exact readout during training (ERL, pilot).** Recomputing the balanced LDA last layer every round (or every 5) and

  bodies is lower (MNIST 89.2 vs 94.9): training under a fixed balanced readout makes the representation slightly

* Earlier negative results (prior-completing batches, lazy decay on top of HWA, the Gaussian and hybrid generators,

## 6. Related work

**Model-heterogeneous FL by distillation and partial sharing.** FedAvg [2] assumes that every client trains the
same architecture, so that parameters can be averaged. A first family of methods replaces parameter averaging with
an exchange of predictions. FedMD [3] and DS-FL [6] share client outputs on a public or unlabelled dataset and
distil the consensus back into each model. FedDF [4] fuses heterogeneous client models at the server by ensemble
distillation on unlabelled or generated inputs. FedGKT [5] moves a large model to the server and trains it by
distilling from small edge models. AvgKD [7] analyses these protocols in a kernel-regression model and shows that
distillation-based fusion degrades under data heterogeneity. A second family shares only part of the network.
LG-FedAvg [8] keeps local representations and averages a global head. FedRep [9] does the reverse: it learns a
shared representation and personal heads. FedProto [10] exchanges class prototypes instead of parameters. GeFL-F
[1] combines both ideas: it shares a frozen feature extractor and a federated feature generator, and it leaves
the heads architecture-specific. Our work keeps GeFL-F's communication pattern and architecture freedom
unchanged. It studies what that pattern does to rare classes when the shared component is a FedAvg-trained
*generator*.

**Generator-based and data-free knowledge transfer.** FedGen [11] trains a lightweight generator in feature space at
the server. The generator is fitted so that the ensemble of client predictors classifies its samples as the
conditioning label, and clients then regularise local training with these samples under that hard label. Its
supplementary variant weights each client's predictor per label by that client's label count. FedFTG [12] uses a
server-side generator to fine-tune the global model by data-free distillation. It samples labels in proportion to
global class counts and weights each client's teacher loss per class by the client's share of that class. DENSE
[13] trains a generator against the client ensemble and distils the ensemble into a global model in one round.
FedCG [14] has clients share conditional GANs in place of their private extractors. FedCVAE [15] fits client
conditional VAEs for one-shot FL under extreme heterogeneity. Felo/Velo [16] trains a server-side conditional VAE
on clients' mid-level features and sends synthetic features and per-class averaged logits back to heterogeneous
clients. FedVTC [17] shares feature mean and covariance statistics, from which clients generate data to fine-tune
heterogeneous models; it is the closest precedent for a generator driven by federated class statistics. GeFL [1]
trains the generator itself by FedAvg across clients, with CVAE, DCGAN or DDPM
backbones, in image space (GeFL) or feature space (GeFL-F). FedGen and FedFTG apply label-count weights to the
*teacher predictions or losses* that train a server-side generator. HWA instead weights the *parameters* of a
client-trained generator, namely its per-class conditioning rows, during aggregation. It is derived from a failure
that is specific to that setting: under FedAvg with Adam's coupled weight decay, non-holders shrink a class's
row at Adam's step size while holders barely grow it, so tail rows contract even when most clients hold the class. Closely related ideas exist elsewhere: federated recommendation aggregates each item embedding over the clients that updated it (FedNCF, Perifanis & Efraimidis, Knowledge-Based Systems 2022), and class embeddings can collapse under FedAvg when clients see only positive labels (FedAwS, Yu et al., ICML 2020), a different cause from decay. Class-conditional collapse in centralised generators is also known (Shahbazi et al., ICLR 2022; Qin et al., CVPR 2023; Rangwani et al., ECCV 2022). Per-class aggregation over the clients that hold a class also appears in cwFedAvg [29] and in FedProto's
prototype averaging [10]; HWA applies the same rule to a generator's class rows. What we add is the federated
mechanism: coupled Adam decay on non-holders combined with flat averaging.

**FL under class imbalance and long tails.** Much of this work locates the bias in the classifier. CCVR [18]
re-calibrates the classifier after training on virtual features sampled from a Gaussian mixture built from
federated feature statistics. Our federated Gaussian sufficient-statistics generator is close to this idea. CReFF
[19] re-trains the classifier on "federated features" optimised so that their gradients match those of real data.
FEDIC [20] uses ensemble distillation with logit adjustment and a calibration gating network. A second group
changes the local loss. Wang et al. [22] infer each round's class composition with a monitor and counter it with
a Ratio Loss. FedLC [21]
calibrates logits by each client's label frequencies through pairwise margins. FedRS [27] restricts softmax updates
for classes a client does not hold, and MAP [28] develops this restricted-softmax view for incomplete classes.
BalanceFL [24] changes local training so that the uploaded model behaves as if trained on balanced data. CLIMB
[23] casts class imbalance as a constrained learning problem. Fed-GraB [25] re-weights gradients using an
estimate of the global long-tailed prior, and FedLoGe [26] combines a shared backbone with a static ETF
classifier and per-client classifiers. Class-aware aggregation also appears for classifier layers: cwFedAvg [29]
averages per class with weights given by client class distributions, and FedLA [30] weights client updates using
both data size and label distribution. Our LA component is a direct application of logit adjustment by the client's
own prior. It is closely related to FedLC and to the logit adjustment in FEDIC, and we do not claim it as new on its
own; FedRoD [49] trains a balanced-softmax head with client counts, which is the same correction. What is new is
the setting, a generator and heads trained in separate phases, and the finding that LA helps only after the generator
is repaired. Post-hoc recalibration from federated statistics is established: CCVR [18], Fed3R [50] (closed-form
ridge classifiers from federated sums) and FedNCM [51] (nearest-class-mean heads) all compute a classifier from
aggregated class statistics, and FedNPC [52] calibrates federated long-tailed classifiers after training. These are
the natural baselines for the readout gap of §B.11 and are not yet run. The principle "take class information from
exactly aggregated sums" is therefore not new; our use of it is to anchor and correct a generator.

**Long-tailed recognition.** The class-balanced loss [31] re-weights classes by the effective number of samples
$E(n)=(1-\beta^n)/(1-\beta)$, a saturating model of how much new information extra samples add. LDAM [33] enforces
label-dependent margins and defers re-weighting. Decoupling [34] shows that instance-balanced representations with
a re-balanced classifier are hard to beat. Logit adjustment [32] adds $\tau\log\pi$ to the logits during training
(or subtracts it post hoc), which is Fisher-consistent for the balanced error. We borrow the effective number as an
inverse-variance weight for *holders* of a class, not as a loss weight. We use logit adjustment with a per-client
rather than a global prior, which needs no communication. Our diagnosis also depends on the difference between
coupled and decoupled weight decay in Adam [43].

**Synthetic data, label quality and soft labels.** Knowledge distillation [35] trains a student on a teacher's soft
predictions, and label smoothing [36] mixes the one-hot target with a uniform distribution, which can remove
inter-class information useful to a student [37]. Menon et al. [38] show that a teacher that approximates Bayes
class-probabilities lowers the variance of the student's objective, with a bias–variance trade-off when the
approximation is poor. Work on training classifiers with generated data finds that label fidelity of samples, not
only visual quality, limits accuracy. The Classification Accuracy Score [39] trains on generated samples and shows
large drops relative to real data, with class-specific failures. Later studies of diffusion-generated training data
report gains from synthetic data together with clear limits [40, 41, 42]. CSL applies this view to generated
*features* in heterogeneous FL. Its target mixes the one-hot label with the mean softmax of all architectures' heads,
so it is label smoothing toward a learned ensemble posterior rather than toward a uniform distribution. The
bias–variance argument of [38] explains why the mixture ($\beta=0.5$) beats the pure ensemble ($\beta=1$), and why
lower-bias labels make a larger synthetic budget useful. Unlike FedDF, DENSE or our negative server-side variant,
CSL does not distil at the server. It only relabels the clients' synthetic phase. Feature-space generators for
classifiers are established outside FL (f-CLSWGAN [53], Delta-encoder [54]), and a generator trained by MMD against
random-feature class embeddings is DP-MERF [55], which our server-only KME-Gen reproduces on federated sums.

**Privacy.** Secure aggregation [44] lets a server learn only the sum of client vectors. HWA needs only the sums
$\sum_k E(n_{k,c})\,w_c^{(k)}$ and $\sum_k E(n_{k,c})$, so it runs inside such a protocol and reveals neither
individual rows nor individual histograms. Differential privacy [45] and its use in deep learning [46] and in
federated training at the user or client level [47, 48] give formal guarantees. Our DP experiments add Laplace
noise to class counts (minimal variant) and Gaussian noise to the four statistics the anchored method adds (E26).
Neither covers the generator's FedAvg training or the heads; a formal accounting for those is left to future work.
Secure aggregation is simulated in our code: the server is handed the sum.

### 6.7 Positioning relative to the closest works

GeFL/GeFL-F [1] was evaluated only with IID clients and aggregates every generator parameter by flat FedAvg. We
show that, combined with Adam's coupled weight decay, this contracts the conditioning rows of tail classes even
when most clients hold them (Theorem 1), and HWA removes exactly the non-holder term while reducing to FedAvg when
class counts are equal. FedGen [11] and FedFTG [12] also use label-count weights, but on the teacher predictions or losses that train
a *server-side* generator; we weight the *parameters* of a *client-trained* generator. Both label generated samples
with the hard conditioning label, whereas CSL relabels them with a one-hot/cross-architecture-consensus mixture.
CReFF [19] and CCVR [18] use synthetic or virtual features to re-train one homogeneous classifier after training,
while we keep per-architecture heads and change how the generator is aggregated and its samples labelled throughout
training. FedLC [21] already debiases local training with client label frequencies; our LA belongs to the same
family, and our claim for it is only empirical: it is needed, but it pays off fully only after HWA. FedDF-style
server distillation [4] of the ensemble on generated features lowers IID accuracy in our hands. CSL uses the same
ensemble only as a low-variance label inside the clients' synthetic phase, and this is what makes a larger
synthetic budget pay off. The closest labelling scheme is Felo/Velo [16]. Its server averages clients' logits
*per class* (one vector per class, computed on real data) and uses them as KL targets on clients' *real* samples; its
generated features serve only as MSE targets for the feature extractor. CSL instead labels *each generated feature*
with the consensus of all architectures' heads on that very feature, and trains the heads on it. The per-sample
consensus is what tracks the generator's per-sample label error, the bias $b$ of Proposition 3.

## 7. Limitations

* **Margins.** On new seeds with validation-chosen settings, our margin over the strongest baseline is 0.5–2.0
  points (significant on MNIST, SVHN and CIFAR-100, not on FashionMNIST or CIFAR-10); the pre-registered ≥ 2-point
  prediction failed. The 8–21 point gains over un-recalibrated GeFL-F are mostly a readout and optimiser effect.
* **Readout protocol.** The strong (all-client) readouts need each architecture's body broadcast once to every client;
  the published per-architecture protocols are much weaker at K = 10 for every method.
* **Absolute accuracy on hard data.** With the frozen 10-channel extractor, CIFAR-100-LT accuracy is 15–19% for every
  method; CIFAR-10 long tail is about 54%.
* **Selection on the test set.** For seeds 0–2, $T_s$, $\beta$, $\tau$, the gate threshold and the choice among
  variants were made on the reported test sets. K17 (new seeds, validation-selected, Holm-corrected) re-tests the
  main comparison; $\beta$, $\tau$ and the gate threshold were still fixed from the earlier runs.
* **HWA's contribution is modest.** Once coupled decay is removed, HWA adds 1–2 points, significant only on MNIST at
  50 and 100 clients.
* **Reproducibility.** Seeds fix the splits and initialisations, but GPU training was not deterministic; a
  `--deterministic` option and the code commit are recorded for runs from now on.
* **Scale of the federation.** At K = 10 each architecture has one client, so per-architecture aggregation of heads
  is trivial there. The rarest classes are held by 6–8 of 10 clients, the rare classes are always 6–9, and CIFAR-10's
  realised imbalance is 48:1, not 100:1.
* **Frozen extractor and BatchNorm.** The extractor is trained once and frozen, as in GeFL-F; its BatchNorm statistics
  are averaged by FedAvg like any other parameter. A trainable or non-IID-robust extractor is out of scope.
* **Metric.** The paper's `best_mean_acc` is a best-over-rounds test metric. We report final-round values beside it;
  on CIFAR-10 IID the difference decides whether there is a gain at all.
* **Gate.** The generator gate's threshold was set after seeing SVHN, and CIFAR-10 lies near it with only one branch
  run.
* **Balanced data.** In balanced (IID) data the methods are within 0.6 points once both use a readout; on CIFAR-10
  the minimal variant is slightly ahead of ours. Under label skew it is better on MNIST, FashionMNIST and CIFAR-10, but not on
  SVHN, where the anchor is uninformative and the gate falls back to the class rows.
* **Upper bound.** Every method is bounded by the frozen shared feature extractor, which GeFL-F's protocol fixes. On
  CIFAR-10 this bound (oracle ≈ 50 under the long tail) is far below the image-space GeFL numbers in the paper.
* **What the statistics reveal.** The exact statistics are federation-level sums, plus counts. Classes held by a
  single client are not hidden by secure aggregation; DP noise (§B.6) is the remedy, at a measured cost, and it covers
  only the added statistics.

* **The generator gate is set from four datasets.** $F$ separates SVHN from MNIST and FashionMNIST by more than a
  factor of ten, but CIFAR-10 is within a factor of two of the threshold, and a dataset with intermediate separation
  would need both branches evaluated. Under the gate, the rows branch inherits HWA's
  cost (aggregated class counts).
* Results are on small images and on the paper's backbone sizes. Larger FEs could change the generator's failure
  profile.
* HWA uses aggregated class counts. LCD is the fully local alternative but is weaker.
* CSL's gain in IID data is small (+0.5 to +0.7 points), although it is consistent across seeds.
* MND in feature space is a proxy for privacy, not a guarantee. A formal DP accounting of the generator itself is
  future work.

  differences are not resolved). This is consistent with the damage coming from *non-holders'* decay.
* *CSL.* It matters where the generator's labels are poor (MNIST: −2.5 without it) and not where they are decent
  (FashionMNIST).
* *LA temperature.* τ = 2 helps FashionMNIST (+1.4) and slightly hurts MNIST. BBC captures this residual bias
  without tuning.

**Privacy.**
* *DP on class counts (minimal variant).* Laplace noise at ε = 1 on every client's class histogram costs 0.2
  (FashionMNIST) and 1.3 points (MNIST). At ε = 0.1 the method is still 11–14 points above GeFL-F. This covers the
  counts HWA uses, not the generator or the heads.
* *Memorisation.* Feature-space MND equals GeFL-F's.

**Formal DP on every statistic the anchored method adds (E26; long tail, K = 10, 3 seeds; balanced accuracy).**

| | MNIST | FashionMNIST |
|---|---|---|
| GeFL-F (no formal privacy) | 75.25 | 58.47 |
| final method, exact statistics | 94.82 | 79.67 |
| final method, $(8, 10^{-5})$-DP statistics | 90.36 | 72.77 |
| final method, $(2, 10^{-5})$-DP statistics | 85.06 | 65.59 |

* *Mechanism.* Features are clipped to the 90th-percentile norm of the public held-out pool. Each of the four
  releases (counts, sums of $h$, sums of $\|h\|^2$, sums of $\phi(h)$) gets Gaussian noise, and the budget is split
  equally under zCDP. Prototypes, MC and KH targets, and the BBC gate are post-processing.
* *Gating noisy classes.* Where a class's noisy statistic would be too noisy to help, MC and KH fall back to the plain
  generator for that class. This is decided from the noisy counts, at no extra cost. MC is applied to 7 of 10 classes
  at $\varepsilon = 8$ and to 5 at $\varepsilon = 2$.
* *Cost.* 4.5–6.9 points at $\varepsilon = 8$ and 9.8–14.1 at $\varepsilon = 2$, almost all on the rarest classes,
  whose 24 samples cannot be both private and accurate. BBC no longer helps under DP.
* *Still ahead, but not everywhere significantly.* At $\varepsilon = 2$ the method is +9.8 above GeFL-F on MNIST and
  +7.1 on FashionMNIST, where the difference is not significant with 3 seeds ($p = 0.084$).
* *Scope of the guarantee.* The DP covers only the four added statistics, per example, with add/remove neighbours
  and a trusted party adding the noise. The generator's FedAvg training, the heads, the consensus-label sums and the
  gate's inputs are not covered, in either method, and secure aggregation is simulated (the server is given the sum).

### B.7 What did not work

These ideas were tested on a reduced schedule first and dropped on the evidence. We report them because they sharpen
what the working method actually fixes.

* **Prior-completing mixed batches.** These fill a client's missing classes with synthetic features, which works only
  when tail synthetic features are faithful. Before HWA they are not; FMNIST went from 65.2 to 59.6 with LA.
* **Lazy conditioning decay.** It removes the collapse from the client side, with no shared counts. With HWA present
  it adds nothing (78.6 vs 79.1). It remains the fallback when even aggregated counts are disallowed.
* **A federated Gaussian generator (exact aggregation of sufficient statistics).** It was competitive on MNIST
  (87.0 vs 87.9). It is worse in IID data and fails on SVHN (≈ 45 vs 62), where tail fidelity is 0.14: a single
  Gaussian per class cannot model real feature distributions.
* **A hybrid CVAE/Gaussian generator.** The quick pass favoured it; the full schedule reversed that (87.1 vs 87.9).
  This shows why a quick pass alone is not enough to decide.
* **Server-side classifier re-calibration, and gap-filling synthetic labels.** Both are weaker than LA.
* **Server-side ensemble distillation on generated features (SED).**
  * *Motivation.* The ensemble of the G heads beats the mean head by 3.2 points on FMNIST IID, 4.5 on MNIST LT and
    7.9 on SVHN IID. SED distils that ensemble into every head at the server on fresh generated features, using
    CSL's target, with no client cost.
  * *Result (FMNIST quick pass).* It *lowers* IID accuracy (81.69 → 80.96) and raises the long tail only a little
    (70.65 → 71.30).
  * *Why.* The bound $R_{\text{real}} \le R_{\text{syn}} + \ell_{\max}\,\mathrm{TV}(p_{\text{syn}}, p_{\text{real}})$
    explains it. In IID data the heads are already good, so the generator's infidelity dominates what distillation
    adds. The ensemble's knowledge can reach the heads only through features as faithful as the generator's.
  * *Implication.* The remaining IID gap to the paper's best FMNIST number (DDPM-F) is a *generator* gap. §B.8 tests
    exactly that.

* **Bayesian consensus labels.**
  * *Idea.* Replace CSL's fixed mixture by the Bayes posterior under symmetric generator noise, with the generator's
    fidelity estimated without labels from ensemble agreement.
  * *Result.* Worse than CSL: 83.16 vs 84.22 on FashionMNIST IID ($p = 0.004$), and 87.3 vs 90.5 on MNIST LT.
  * *Why.* The estimate is circular. The ensemble was trained on the generator's own samples, so it agrees with
    their labels ($\hat\rho \to 0.999$), and the target collapses to the hard label.
  * *Lesson.* CSL works as *distillation* of the ensemble's class-similarity structure, not as label correction.
* **Held-class real–synthetic alignment.** Aligning each head's embedding of synthetic features to that of real
  features on the classes a client holds *increased* the synthetic–real gap on unheld classes. Dropped.

### B.8 Generator-agnostic: our method on DDPM-F, the paper's best feature generator

The best FMNIST result of any GeFL variant in the paper is 84.28, from GeFL-F with the feature diffusion model
DDPM-F. Our components do not depend on the generator:
* HWA averages the class columns of DDPM-F's two context-embedding layers over holders.
* LA and CSL act on the heads.

We ported the authors' DDPM-F unchanged: the ddpm16 ContextUnet, $n_T = 200$, a linear $\beta$ schedule from
$10^{-4}$ to 0.02, context dropout 0.1, and Adam at $10^{-4}$ decayed linearly to 0 over $T_{KA}$ rounds, then frozen.
It runs GeFL-F, + CSL and Ours on it. Every round, all clients' synthetic phases draw from one shared pool of fresh
uniform-label samples. For each head this is exactly the reference's per-client draw in distribution, at 1/K of the
sampling cost, and it is the same for every method compared.

On FMNIST IID (`best_mean_acc`), GeFL-F on DDPM-F scores 83.16 (seed 0 only), not the published 84.28. + CSL scores
83.04 (seed 0) and Ours 82.82 ± 0.39 (seeds 0–1). In our hands DDPM-F is only +0.28 over CVAE-F on the same seed, and
CSL does not add to it: diffusion samples are more diverse but less class-pure, with referee fidelity 0.55 against
0.86. On SVHN IID (K04, 3 seeds) Ours on DDPM-F scores 70.37 against 69.77 for GeFL-F on DDPM-F, both well below the
CVAE-F pipeline (75.82). So the components *run* on DDPM-F, but we have no evidence that they help it; the section
title's "generator-agnostic" means only the former.

### B.9 Consensus labels make a larger synthetic budget useful (E16, FMNIST IID, 3 seeds)

**Prediction.** Train a head on real data plus a share $\lambda$ of synthetic data. Its error is roughly
$\sigma^2/(n_{\text{real}}+\lambda n_{\text{syn}}) + \lambda^2 b(t)^2$, where $b(t)$ is the bias of the synthetic label
against the true posterior of a generated feature. The optimal $\lambda$ grows as $b$ shrinks. Hard labels have a large
$b$, so the paper finds $T_s = 5$ no better than $T_s = 1$ (its Fig. 10). CSL lowers $b$, so with CSL more synthetic
epochs should help.

| `best_mean_acc` | $T_s=1$ | $T_s=3$ | $T_s=5$ | $T_s=10$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GeFL-F (hard labels) | 82.96 | 83.20 | 83.18 | – |
| + CSL | 83.31 | 83.90 | 84.14 | **84.37** (84.34 / 84.16 / 84.62) |
| Ours (HWA + LA + CSL) | 83.21 | – | 84.12 | **84.34** (84.55 / 83.88 / 84.59; p = 0.002 vs GeFL-F) |

**The same budgets under the long tail** (FMNIST, IF = 100, Dir 0.5, 3 seeds, final balanced accuracy):

| | GeFL-F | Ours, $T_s=1$ | Ours, $T_s=5$ | Ours, $T_s=10$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Balanced accuracy | 59.13 | 76.94 | 78.76 | **79.23** |
| Tail recall | 36.04 | 70.22 | 71.92 | 71.83 |
| Worst class | 20.36 | 29.38 | 32.11 | 32.79 |

Under the long tail the larger budget also helps: +2.29 over $T_s = 1$, and +20.1 over GeFL-F. On MNIST under the
long tail it does not (90.20 / 90.78 / 90.15). There, HWA has made
the tail features faithful and CSL has lowered their label bias. This is the synergy of §4.3, seen through the
budget.

* The interaction appears exactly as predicted.
* At $T_s = 10$, CSL with the cheap CVAE-F generator reaches the paper's best FMNIST number of *any* variant, 84.28
  (GeFL-F with feature diffusion), on `best_mean_acc` (final round: 84.32). We have no equivalence test, so this is a
  numerical match against a single published value, not a demonstrated tie.
* MNIST is near its ceiling (oracle ≈ 97.2), and a larger budget does not help there.
* With one fixed setting ($T_s = 5$), the full method gives 84.12 on FMNIST and 96.6 on MNIST. With $T_s = 10$ the
  full method gives 84.34 on FMNIST, a tie with the paper's best (84.28).

### B.12 Cost

* *Minimal variant.* Summed over all stages, HWA + LA + CSL takes 373 s per run against GeFL-F's 366 s (+1.9%),
  measured on MNIST under the long tail, averaged over 3 seeds. HWA adds $C$ numbers per client per round; CSL adds
  the per-round head or pool-prediction upload of §4.3 and one broadcast of soft labels.
* *Ours-A.* End to end it is 2.2–2.8× slower than GeFL-F. The head stage (Stage III) is 5–14× slower, and kernel
  herding accounts for 83–88% of it.
* *Bytes.* The PC-VAE has 6.93M parameters on CIFAR-10 against 5.61M for the CVAE-F (+23% per generator upload). The
  exact statistics (counts, sums of $h$, of $\|h\|^2$ and of $\phi(h)$) take 0.28–0.35 MB per client in two
  secure-aggregation rounds. MC-S adds a broadcast $D \times D$ pooled covariance (5.0–28.8 MB).
