\section{Study design}\label{sec:setup}

The study tests the analysis of \cref{sec:analysis} and the method of \cref{sec:method} on the problem of
\cref{sec:setting}: clients with different head architectures and long-tailed, skewed label distributions. This
section states the research questions, the system under test, the data, the methods compared, the metrics, the
protocol, the ablations, the statistical analysis and the threats to validity.

\subsection{Research questions}\label{sec:setup-rq}

\Cref{tab:rq} lists the questions and the experiment that answers each.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1 (diagnosis).} Where does GeFL-F lose the rare classes under long-tailed label skew? The analysis
names three places: the generator's class rows, the heads' last layers and the synthetic phase. RQ1 checks each in
GeFL-F's own runs.
\item \textbf{RQ2 (effect).} Does \ours raise balanced accuracy over GeFL-F and the other methods that work with
different client models, with every model evaluated as trained?
\item \textbf{RQ3 (representation or last layer).} How much of the difference remains when every method of the
GeFL-F pipeline gets the same exact readout, so that only the heads' bodies differ?
\item \textbf{RQ4 (components).} Which changes to GeFL-F carry the effect?
\item \textbf{RQ5 (scope).} Does the effect hold with natural class imbalance, with other client numbers, label skews
and imbalance ratios, and in the standard protocol with one model shared by all clients?
\end{itemize}
Cost and calibration are reported for every method as secondary outcomes.

\subsection{System under evaluation}\label{sec:setup-system}

The system is GeFL-F's three-stage pipeline (\cref{sec:setting}). In Stage I the clients train a shared feature
extractor and their heads by federated averaging; the extractor is then frozen, and every image's features are
computed once without augmentation. In Stage II the clients train a conditional feature generator by federated
averaging. In Stage III every client trains its head, first on generated features and then on its real features,
and the heads of each architecture are averaged.

\ours keeps this pipeline and changes five things (\cref{sec:method}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item the generator, which is anchored to the exact class means, or uses class rows averaged by CCWA when the gate
finds the classes poorly separated;
\item the moment correction of generated features;
\item logit adjustment in the real phase;
\item consensus soft labels in the synthetic phase;
\item the number of synthetic epochs per round, $T_s=10$ instead of GeFL-F's 1.
\end{enumerate}
The exact readout of \cref{sec:method-readout} is computed after training. We report it separately for every method
of the pipeline and never mix it into the numbers as trained. \Cref{tab:hyper} lists the settings of \ours, which are
the same on every dataset.

The extractor is one convolutional block followed by $2\times2$ max pooling: one $3\times3$ convolution with three
output channels on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST, SVHN, OrganAMNIST, EMNIST and FER-2013, and two convolutions with three and
ten output channels on CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100, DermaMNIST, BloodMNIST, PathMNIST and GTSRB, each convolution followed by
batch normalisation and ReLU. The ten heads, CNN-1 to CNN-10, have one to four convolutional blocks of different widths and a linear
last layer (\cref{app:protocol}). They follow GeFL-F~\cite{kang2025gefl}; the widths do not increase monotonically from
CNN-1 to CNN-10.

\subsection{Datasets and client splits}\label{sec:setup-data}

\paragraph{Long-tailed benchmarks.} \R{main/datasets_cite} are made long-tailed as in GeFL-F's
code~\cite{kang2025gefl}. A fraction of the training set is divided over the classes by the profile of
\cref{sec:setting} with $\rho=100$. The fraction is GeFL-F's on MNIST, Fashion-MNIST and SVHN (0.1) and on CIFAR-10
(0.5). GeFL does not use CIFAR-100; there we take 0.22, which gives the usual CIFAR-100-LT shape. No class receives more
images than remain after the validation images are held out. On CIFAR-10-LT and CIFAR-100-LT the profile asks for more
than that for the largest classes, which are therefore capped. So the realised ratio between the largest and the
smallest class is below 100 (\cref{tab:datasets}); we name every setting by its $\rho$. The class index orders the
classes from the most to the least frequent, so the tail classes are the same in every run.

\paragraph{Natural imbalance.} \R{nat/datasets_cite} keep their own class sizes, with training sets capped at 20,000
images. Their classes are relabelled by training frequency. On three of them the realised ratio is small
(\cref{tab:datasets}): their results speak to label skew more than to a long tail.

\paragraph{Splits.} The training images are split among $K=10$ clients by a Dirichlet draw with $\alpha=0.5$ per
class. The draw is repeated until every client holds at least ten images. Client $k$ trains head architecture $k$, so
in the main setting each architecture has one client.

A held-out block of training images that no client holds forms a validation set: $\min(200,\lfloor 2000/C\rfloor)$
images per class, and at most a quarter of a class with natural imbalance. It has two uses: it trains the referee
classifier of the generator diagnostics, and it sets the public bounds by which secure aggregation scales the
released vectors. It is never used to select a model or a hyperparameter. The other unused training images are
discarded. Every result is on the official test set. A seed fixes the split, so all methods see the same clients;
we checked that the class counts of every client are identical across methods for every dataset and seed.

\subsection{Protocols}\label{sec:setup-protocols}

\paragraph{Main setting: different client models.} This is GeFL-F's setting with $K=10$ clients, one per
architecture. The rounds of the three stages are GeFL-F's: 50, 200 and 100 on CIFAR-10, and the same on CIFAR-100
(\cref{app:protocol} lists every dataset). All federated methods of this setting share the same Stage-I extractor for
a given dataset and seed: Stage I is computed once and reused.\whentab{k50_bal}{ GeFL's second setting has $K=50$
clients, five per architecture (\cref{sec:res-main}); every method of the main comparison is run there too.}

\paragraph{One shared model.} We follow the FedLTLib protocol~\cite{lin2026fedltlib}: CIFAR-10-LT and CIFAR-100-LT with
the standard profile $n_c=\lfloor n_{\max}\rho^{-c/(C-1)}\rfloor$ at $\rho\in\{100,50,10\}$, where $n_{\max}$ is the
number of images per class left after 200 per class (20 on CIFAR-100) are held out for validation. Rounding down gives
the smallest CIFAR-100 class 4 images at $\rho=100$.

There are 20 clients, Dirichlet $\alpha=0.5$, and 8 clients (40\%) take part in each round. All share one
ResNet-8~\cite{he2016resnet}, trained for 200 rounds of 10 local epochs with batch size 32 and SGD (learning rate 0.1,
momentum 0.9, decay 0.99 per round). The baselines run the authors' LongTailLib training code unchanged, on our
splits; we evaluate them every fifth round and score Fed-GraB with its trained classifier.

For \ours the ResNet-8 is split after its second stage into a frozen extractor and a head. The extractor is trained
for 100 rounds with the protocol's local updates, the generator for 100 rounds, and the head for 100 rounds with 10
real epochs per round; the learning-rate decay continues from the extractor's rounds. So \ours uses the baselines'
200 training rounds plus 100 generator rounds. Its head trains on frozen features of images without augmentation,
whereas the baselines train end to end with augmentation. With one architecture, the consensus soft label is the
current shared model's own prediction. \ours averages the clients' models with equal weights, as in the main setting;
the benchmark's FedAvg weights them by their numbers of samples. The clients of each round are a seeded subset; the
library draws its own, so participation is not paired between \ours and the baselines.

\subsection{Methods compared}\label{sec:setup-methods}

\Cref{tab:baselines} lists every method, its reference and the origin of its code, and \cref{tab:fairness} what each
method of the main setting exchanges and computes. The methods fall into six groups, each chosen for one question.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{GeFL-F}, the method \ours extends: the difference to it answers RQ2 directly.
\item \textbf{FedDF}~\cite{lin2020feddf}, ensemble distillation on the server. As in GeFL, the distillation runs on
generated features instead of public data. It is the strongest way to use the same generator without changing it.
\item \textbf{Per-client long-tail losses}: Balanced Softmax~\cite{ren2020bsm}, FedLC~\cite{zhang2022fedlc} and
FedRS~\cite{li2021fedrs}. They test whether a correction of the loss alone repairs the long tail. They are applied
to GeFL-F without its generator. At $K=10$ each architecture has one client, so their heads train on that client's
real features only and exchange nothing in Stage III.
\item \textbf{GeFL's grouped baselines}\whentab{k50_bal}{}: FedAvg~\cite{mcmahan2017fedavg},
FedProx~\cite{li2020fedprox}, FedALA~\cite{zhang2023fedala} and AvgKD~\cite{afonin2022avgkd} run within each
architecture group, as GeFL compares them~\cite{kang2025gefl}. CCVR~\cite{luo2021ccvr} and FedETF~\cite{li2023fedetf},
two long-tail methods, are applied the same way.
\item \textbf{Model-heterogeneous methods from HtFLlib}~\cite{zhang2025htfllib}: prototypes (FedProto, FedTGP), a
generator of embeddings (FedGen), a shared global head (FedGH), shared last layers (LG-FedAvg) and shared
sub-networks (FedMRL). They represent the other ways in which clients with different models exchange knowledge.
\item \textbf{References}: Local, in which each client trains its extractor and head alone, is the reference without
federation.\whennum{main/cifar100/Centralised (pooled data)/bal}{ The pooled-data reference trains the same
architectures with cross-entropy on all training data pooled, without a generator and without a long-tail
correction.}
\end{itemize}
Methods designed for one shared model (\cref{tab:baselines}, lower part) are compared in the one-shared-model setting
only.

\paragraph{Settings and tuning.} No method was tuned on our data, but the settings come from different sources:
\begin{itemize}
\item the HtFLlib methods use the values of HtFLlib's run scripts (SGD, learning rate 0.01, batch 10, feature
dimension 512), with 5 local epochs per round to match our head stage;
\item the LongTailLib methods use the benchmark's common settings, which differ from some methods' own papers;
\item the grouped baselines use the settings of GeFL's code (FedAvg, FedProx with $\mu=0.01$, AvgKD) and of the
authors' code (FedALA) or LongTailLib (CCVR, FedETF);
\item Balanced Softmax, FedLC ($\tau=1$) and FedRS ($\alpha=0.5$) are re-implemented losses with the client's
add-one smoothed class counts;
\item FedDF uses 20 server distillation steps per round (\cref{app:protocol}).
\end{itemize}
The settings of \ours (\cref{tab:hyper}) were fixed during its development, on earlier runs of the same benchmarks
with other seeds (0--2), and were not changed for the runs reported here. This favours \ours over methods that were
not tuned (\cref{sec:setup-threats}).

\subsection{Metrics}\label{sec:setup-metrics}

Let $r_{gc}$ be the recall of class $c$ by the model of architecture $g$ on the test set, which is balanced on every
long-tailed benchmark. The primary metric is the balanced accuracy, $\frac1G\sum_g\frac1C\sum_c r_{gc}$: the mean
over the $G=10$ client models of each model's mean class recall. GeFL~\cite{kang2025gefl} reports the mean over the
ten models in the same way. It is reported with the heads as trained and, for the methods of the GeFL-F pipeline,
with the exact readout. The second number answers RQ3.

The other metrics are:
\begin{itemize}
\item the recall of the tail classes, the last 40\% by frequency rank, and of the worst class,
$\min_c\frac1G\sum_g r_{gc}$, which is the lowest class recall of the recall averaged over the models;
\item the number of classes that no model recognises (pooled recall zero);
\item macro-F1, macro precision and the multi-class Matthews correlation coefficient~\cite{gorodkin2004mcc};
\item the geometric mean of the class recalls, each floored at 1\%, computed for each model and then averaged;
\item macro AUC-PR;
\item the expected calibration error with 15 bins~\cite{guo2017calibration} and its class-wise
form~\cite{kull2019classwise};
\item the balanced accuracy of the weakest architecture;
\item the accuracy on each client's own class mix: the test accuracy re-weighted to the class frequencies of that
model's clients.
\end{itemize}
Every per-class metric is logged for every class at every evaluation.

GeFL's code reports each model's best test accuracy over the evaluated rounds. We report the model after the last
round, which involves no selection on the test set. GeFL's measure is given in \cref{tab:app-gefl}.

Cost is measured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item communication: the bytes of the model and statistics messages per client, with a secure sum counted as eight
bytes per summed value and client, without the protocol's key and share messages, and without evaluation traffic;
\item time: the wall-clock time of a run on a T4 GPU, which in most notebooks ran a second run at the same time;
\item computation: forward floating-point operations per image, with training counted as three forward passes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Protocol and implementation}\label{sec:setup-impl}

Every configuration is run with seeds 3, 4 and 5; settings that came back with fewer seeds say so. A seed fixes the
split and the initialisation of every method, so runs with the same seed are paired. Models are evaluated every five
rounds on the test set, before and after aggregation. The reported model is the one after the last round.

\ours and every method of the main setting run in Flower 1.42~\cite{beutel2020flower}, a simulation with the Ray
backend, one simulation per run. The one-shared-model baselines run in the authors' LongTailLib code on the same
client splits and are evaluated on the same test set. All runs used Kaggle notebooks with two NVIDIA T4 GPUs and two
runs per GPU at a time.

All releases of \ours use Flower's SecAgg+~\cite{bell2020secaggplus}, with 30-bit stochastic quantisation of every
vector after division by a public bound, four times the largest value on the validation features. A value beyond the
bound would be clipped.

The Flower implementation was checked against our earlier, single-process implementation, which follows GeFL's
code. The client splits are identical, every client training step agrees weight for weight in unit tests, and the
balanced accuracy agrees within the larger of two points and three seed standard deviations (\cref{app:protocol}).

GPU computation is not bit-reproducible (cuDNN autotuning, CUDA graphs), so two runs with the same seed differ. A
notebook run twice with the same run list (\R{repeat/dataset}, \R{repeat/bal/n} runs) measures this. The
standard deviation of the difference between the two runs of one configuration is \R{repeat/bal/sd} points of
balanced accuracy as trained and \R{repeat/er/sd} with the exact readout.

\subsection{Ablations}\label{sec:setup-ablation}

Two sets of runs separate the components (RQ4), on CIFAR-10-LT and CIFAR-100-LT with the same splits and seeds as the
main comparison.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Variants of GeFL-F} test the diagnosis: GeFL-F without its generator (what the generator adds at all),
GeFL-F with decoupled decay and logit adjustment (the cheapest repair of the first two losses), GeFL-F with a
Gaussian class-conditional generator from the exact statistics, and FedDF with logit adjustment.
\item \textbf{\ours with one component changed} measures each component's share:
\begin{itemize}
\item class rows with CCWA, or with flat averaging, instead of the gate;
\item no moment correction;
\item no consensus soft labels;
\item no logit adjustment;
\item one synthetic epoch per round instead of ten. This also separates the effect of the longer synthetic phase
from that of the exact statistics.
\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
Each variant is reported as trained and with the exact readout.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}\label{sec:setup-stats}

We report the mean $\pm$ sample standard deviation over seeds. A difference to \ours is the mean paired difference
over the seeds both methods have, with a 95\% $t$ interval and the paired $t$-test. The $p$-values are corrected by
Holm's procedure~\cite{holm1979} over the methods compared in the same table and setting: twelve methods as trained,
and the five methods of the GeFL-F pipeline with the exact readout. There is no correction across datasets or metrics.
"Best" and "strongest other method" are decided on the seeds both methods have.

With three seeds the tests have little power. The run-to-run variation above is of the same order as some of the
differences. We therefore report every interval with its test and treat differences below about one point as
unresolved.

\subsection{Threats to validity}\label{sec:setup-threats}

\paragraph{Internal validity.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Tuning.} The settings of \ours were chosen on earlier runs of the same benchmarks with other seeds and
the same test sets; the baselines were not tuned. The test splits of the reported runs are new, but the choice could
still favour \ours.
\item \textbf{Compute.} \ours trains its heads for ten synthetic epochs per round, against one for GeFL-F and FedDF
and none for the methods without a generator (\cref{tab:fairness}). The ablation with one synthetic epoch measures how
much of the difference this explains.
\item \textbf{Ports.} The HtFLlib methods were designed for end-to-end training and run here on a frozen extractor
with HtFLlib's settings. FedProto and FedTGP are best in their first round and then lose accuracy
(\cref{sec:res-main}), which may mean that these settings do not suit frozen features.
\item \textbf{Non-determinism.} Pairing by seed removes the variation of the split but not that of GPU computation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Construct validity.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Balanced accuracy is a mean over ten models of different sizes, so a large gain of the small heads and a small
gain of the large heads give the same number. \Cref{tab:app-worstarch} gives the weakest architecture.
\item The headline numbers are the heads as trained; the readout is a separate evaluation.
\item The last-round model can be below a method's best round; \cref{tab:app-gefl} gives the best round.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{External validity.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The extractor is one frozen convolutional block and the heads are small CNNs, as in GeFL-F. Absolute
accuracies are therefore low, and the results may not carry over to large pretrained models.
\item The long tail is synthetic on the benchmarks, with the same tail classes in every run.
\item The label skew is a Dirichlet split; feature shift between clients is not studied.
\item The one-shared-model protocol is run at fewer seeds for \ours than planned (\cref{tab:shared}).
\end{itemize}
