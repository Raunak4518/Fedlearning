\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the gains consist of.} A large part of what any method of the GeFL-F family loses under a long tail is
in its last layer, and an exact readout recovers it for every member of the family (\cref{tab:readout}).
\claim{The difference between \ours and the strongest baseline is therefore smaller with the readout than without it;
we report both, and the comparison with the readout is the one that measures the representation.}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The extractor is small and frozen, as in GeFL-F; we did not study pre-trained or larger extractors.
\item In the main setting each architecture has one client ($K=10$). Settings with several clients per
architecture are covered only by \cref{tab:robust}.
\item The released class statistics are exact federation-level sums. For classes with few samples they describe
those samples closely, and the results here do not include formal differential privacy.
\item \claim{At imbalance ratios 50 and 10 in the one-shared-model setting we ran one seed, so those columns carry no
interval.}
\item \ours costs more than GeFL-F (\cref{tab:cost}), mainly through the longer synthetic phase.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Generator-based model-heterogeneous federated learning loses rare classes in three places: the optimiser's treatment
of clients that lack a class, a last layer fitted to one client's prior, and synthetic features that do not carry
their label. Each has an exact description, and each can be repaired with class statistics that secure aggregation
computes exactly. \claim{\ours, which applies these repairs together, improves balanced accuracy and in particular the
recall of the rarest classes across \R{count/datasets} datasets and two protocols.} Its code, logs and every figure's
data are public.
