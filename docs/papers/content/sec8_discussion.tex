\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{What the gains consist of.} A large part of what any method of the GeFL-F family loses under a long tail is
in its last layer, and the exact readout recovers it for every member of the family (\cref{tab:readout}). This is why
the readout is part of \ours, and why most of its margin over the published methods is the readout's.
\claim{When every method of the pipeline has the readout, \ours keeps a lead of \R{main/er_margin_lo} to
\R{main/er_margin_hi} points over the strongest of them on the CIFAR benchmarks, not resolved with three seeds, and a
significant lead over GeFL-F on CIFAR-100-LT.} The comparison with the readout is closer to a comparison of the
bodies, but the readout is the same linear classifier for every body, so it does not measure every property of a
representation. With one shared model the readout lowers the accuracy of \ours (\cref{sec:res-shared}); it is meant
for heads trained on one client each.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
\begin{itemize}
\item The extractor is small and frozen, as in GeFL-F, and shared by all clients, so the clients' architectures
differ only in their heads; we did not study pre-trained or larger extractors, or clients with different backbones.
\item No published method addresses both different client models and a long tail, so the comparison is with
long-tail losses applied per client, with FedDF and with model-heterogeneous methods designed for label skew; the
long-tail methods CCVR and FedETF enter only GeFL's grouped protocol, within each architecture.
\item The splits are pure label skew (\cref{sec:setting}); feature and concept shift between clients are not tested.
\item Three seeds per setting give fragile intervals and paired tests; the tests are reported with Holm's correction,
and differences of about a point should be read with that in mind.
\item In the main setting each architecture has one client ($K=10$)\whentab{robust}{; settings with several clients
per architecture are covered only by \cref{tab:robust}}.
\item Secure aggregation hides each client's contribution from an honest-but-curious server, but the released
federation-level sums are not differentially private: for classes with few samples they describe those samples
closely. \ours should not be called privacy-preserving beyond that protocol guarantee.
\item \claim{Some settings were run with fewer than three seeds; their tables say so and carry no interval.}
\item \ours costs more than GeFL-F (\cref{tab:cost}): its extra time is mostly in the longer synthetic phase of
Stage III, and its extra communication mostly in the training of the generator, which with its projection of the
class means is larger than GeFL-F's. We did not compare the methods at
matched communication or wall-clock time; the ablation with one synthetic epoch shows how much \ours depends on the
longer phase\numelse{abl/cifar10/heads_vs/GeFL-F (T_s=10)}{, and GeFL-F and FedDF were also run with ten synthetic
epochs}{, not what the other methods would reach with it}.
\item The gate threshold $F_0$ and the other settings of \cref{tab:hyper} were chosen on earlier runs of the same
benchmarks, partly with the same seeds and client splits and on the same test sets; they were fixed before these runs
and not swept, and the baselines were not tuned (\cref{sec:setup-threats}).
\item With one model shared by all clients, the strongest long-tail methods (\R{t2/cifar10-r100/above_list} on
CIFAR-10-LT) are better (\cref{tab:shared}); \ours is designed for clients with different models and has no advantage
when their models can be averaged.
\item The absolute accuracy of the main setting is low. GeFL's authors note that GeFL-F is less effective on CIFAR-10
because of the lower performance of its feature extractor and target networks there~\cite{kang2025gefl}. In GeFL's
own setting (a balanced training set divided evenly among 10 clients) our earlier implementation of GeFL-F reaches
\R{repro/cifar10/GeFL-F/final}\% on CIFAR-10 (\R{repro/cifar10/GeFL-F/best}\% with GeFL's best-round measure;
GeFL's Fig.~4 shows about \R{repro/cifar10/published}\%) but only \R{repro/cifar100/GeFL-F/final}\% on CIFAR-100, so on CIFAR-100
the level is set by the design and the data budget, and on CIFAR-10 the long tail, together with the smaller training set it leaves, costs most of the
difference\whennum{main/cifar100/Centralised (pooled data)/bal}{, and the same architectures, trained with cross-entropy on all
the training data pooled (extractor and heads, no generator, no long-tail correction), reach \R{main/cifar10/Centralised (pooled data)/bal} on CIFAR-10-LT and
\R{main/cifar100/Centralised (pooled data)/bal} on CIFAR-100-LT (\R{main/cifar10/Centralised (pooled data)/er_bal} and
\R{main/cifar100/Centralised (pooled data)/er_bal} with the exact readout)}. These numbers
are not comparable with centralised long-tail results for ResNet-32 and larger networks.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Generator-based model-heterogeneous federated learning loses rare classes in at least three places: the optimiser's
treatment of clients that lack a class, a last layer fitted to one client's prior, and synthetic features that do not
carry their label. Each is addressed by a component of \ours that uses class statistics computed by secure
aggregation or, for the heads, logit adjustment and consensus soft labels.\numelse{comp/cifar10/largest}{}{ Which of
these components carry the effect beyond the readout is not yet measured.} \claim{\ours, which applies these repairs together and ends with an exact readout of every last layer, improves
balanced accuracy over the published methods when clients have different models, on CIFAR-10-LT mostly through the
recall of the tail classes. Most of the gain is the readout's: added to every method of the GeFL-F pipeline, it
leaves \ours a lead that three seeds do not resolve. With one model shared by all clients, the strongest long-tail
methods remain better.} Its code, logs and every figure's
data are public.
