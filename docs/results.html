GeFL‑F, ImprovedRare-class collapse in federated feature generators: its cause, its fix, and the evidence

GeFL‑F trains a shared feature generator so that clients with different model architectures can learn from each other. We show that it fails under long-tailed client data, for a reason we derive exactly. We fix that failure with three changes that keep its privacy model, and we validate every comparison against the authors' own code.

Our GeFL‑F = the paper = the authors' code

Within half a point at K = 10, 50, 100. Our baseline is slightly weaker than theirs under the long tail.

Diagnosis (§2). Under flat FedAvg with Adam's coupled weight decay, the class-conditioning rows of GeFL‑F's generator provably shrink for every class held by fewer than half the clients. Rare classes vanish from the generator, and every head is taught from class-agnostic features.

The final method (§14): anchor every stage to exactly aggregated statistics, sums that secure aggregation delivers, instead of federated-averaged parameters.

  1. PC-VAE. The generator is conditioned on the exact federated class mean, so it has no class-specific parameters to collapse.
  2. MC. Each generated class is moved onto its exact mean and spread: the W2-optimal affine correction.
  3. KH. Kernel herding selects the samples whose kernel mean embedding is closest to the class's exact federated embedding.
  4. Heads. Logit adjustment by each client's own prior (LA), cross-architecture consensus soft labels (CSL), and a 10-epoch synthetic budget, which Proposition 3 shows pays off only once the generator is faithful.
  5. BBC. Server-side balanced bias calibration of each head, switched on by the exact global class counts.

The minimal fix for the paper's own generators is HWA: holder-weighted aggregation of the class rows, which removes the collapse from CVAE‑F and DDPM‑F (§3). All components keep GeFL‑F's privacy model, and the method needs no data augmentation. The paper uses augmentation only for image-space GeFL on CIFAR‑10 (Table IV; best 62.67), never for GeFL‑F.

2Where GeFL‑F breaks: rare classes vanish from the generator

A mechanism we derived, then measured.

CVAE‑F conditions on the label through one learned row per class. Under a long tail, a rare class appears on only mr of the K clients. Every other client still decays that row with Adam's coupled weight decay (10−3) and sends it back unchanged by any gradient. Flat FedAvg then averages mr informative copies with K−mr shrunken ones. When mr < K/2 the shrinkage wins, round after round, so the rare-class rows shrink toward zero. The generator then produces head-class features under tail labels, and the headers learn from wrong data.

rowct+1 = 1K [ Σholders (rowct − ηg) + (K−mc)(1−ηλ)S rowct ]  ⇒  shrinks whenever mc < K/2
flat averaging of a class row

Fix (HWA, holder-weighted aggregation): average each class row only over the clients that hold that class, weighted by their sample counts (the counts travel through secure aggregation as sums). Every other parameter is still averaged as in GeFL‑F. The measured result: the tail row norm returns to the head level, and the generator's tail features become recognisable again. Fidelity is the accuracy of an independent referee classifier on generated features, by class group.

Two components carry the gain, and they act on different stages. HWA repairs the generator, so the synthetic phase teaches the tail. 14Final method: anchoring every stage to exact statistics

The method that emerged from the diagnostics: replace federated-averaged parameters by exactly aggregated statistics wherever they decide the result.

PC-VAE conditions the generator on the exact federated class mean, so it has no class-specific parameters to dilute or collapse. MC moves each generated class onto its exact mean and spread: among affine maps, the W2-optimal correction. KH herds the samples toward the exact class kernel embedding, a Frank–Wolfe step on the MMD term of the head's risk bound. BBC fits per-head logit offsets so each head predicts every class equally often on balanced, calibrated synthetic data. It is applied only when the exact global class counts are imbalanced: across 18 runs per regime it added +0.9 (MNIST) and +1.5 (FashionMNIST) under the long tail, and never helped in IID. KME-Gen is the extreme of the same idea, a generator trained only from exact kernel embeddings at the server with no federated training. It is far above GeFL‑F at 1/3000 of the generator communication, but below the learned-decoder variants.

What the three seeds show. At Ts = 10 the full stack is +19.6 (MNIST) and +21.2 (FashionMNIST) points over GeFL‑F, and +4.7 / +0.5 over the best earlier variant, positive on every seed. On MNIST it reaches the accuracy of its own heads with the last layer re-fit on pooled real data. KH helped in all nine paired comparisons (sign test p = 0.004). It works by restoring spread and realistic difficulty: the anchored decoder's raw samples are under-dispersed (0.39–0.49 of the real spread) and, on FashionMNIST, purer than real data. After KH, spread is 0.93–0.98 and IID fidelity equals the referee's own accuracy on real data (0.837 vs 0.839). With the anchored generator, the 10-epoch synthetic budget adds +1.7 on MNIST, where it added nothing to the CVAE. This is the interaction Proposition 3 predicts once the generator's label bias is low. In the paper's IID setting the final method gives 97.62 on MNIST: +1.6 over GeFL‑F (p = 0.001), +0.85 over the CVAE variant, and +1.18 over the best of all ten methods in the paper. On FashionMNIST IID the CVAE + HWA variant remains marginally better (84.34 vs 83.96, p = 0.18): FedAvg does not collapse class rows when every client holds every class.

4The paper's own setting: consensus soft labels

HWA and LA change nothing when data is IID, so we needed a gain there too.

In IID data every client holds every class, so HWA reduces to (nearly) FedAvg and LA's prior is (nearly) uniform. Our method then matches GeFL‑F up to noise. To beat GeFL‑F in its own setting we use a different observation: in the synthetic phase each header learns from features with a hard one-hot label, although a generated feature is often ambiguous. The ten headers are trained on different clients' data with different architectures, so their averaged prediction is a better-calibrated label than the one-hot.

target(x̃) = (1 − β) · onehot(y) + β · 1G Σg softmax(hg(x̃)),   β = 0.5
CSL

The consensus uses only the header outputs on synthetic features, which the server can compute with models it already receives. No real data and no new client message are involved.

4bHow much is left to gain in the IID setting

The FE ceiling explains why the paper's IID numbers are close to each other, and what a fair target is.

The oracle head keeps a head's body but re-fits its last layer on the real features of all clients pooled, with balanced sampling. No federated method can see that data, so it is an optimistic reference for a single head with this FE. In FashionMNIST IID the oracle is about 83.3–84.3, and CSL already reaches 83.2–83.6. The paper's best FashionMNIST number (84.28, DDPM-F) sits at that ceiling. The ensemble of all ten heads is 2–3 points higher, but the paper's metric scores single heads. Distilling the ensemble into each head at the server, on generated features, lowered IID accuracy (§8). The ensemble's knowledge can reach a head only through features as faithful as the generator's.

4cConsensus labels make more synthetic data useful

The paper found that 5 synthetic epochs per round are no better than 1. We predicted that this is a hard-label effect, and that CSL removes it.

A head trained on real data plus a share λ of synthetic data has error of roughly σ²/(nreal + λnsyn) + λ²b(t)². The second term is the bias of the synthetic labels t against the true posterior of each generated feature, and more samples do not average it away. The best λ grows as b shrinks. With hard labels b is large, so extra synthetic epochs do not help; this is the paper's own Fig. 10. CSL's consensus target lowers b, so the optimum moves toward more synthetic data.

Being rerun

In the first K03 run, the rows using consensus labels (+CSL, Ours, FSG+LA+CSL) had a flaw at K = 50 and 100. The several clients of each architecture all trained on the same synthetic slice, then were averaged, which cuts synthetic diversity per architecture 5–10×. This explains why CSL's effect turned negative as K grew. It is fixed (each client now gets its own slice). The affected rows are being rerun on Kaggle together with Ts = 10, and will replace these numbers. K = 10 results are unaffected: there each architecture has one client.

Every variant beats GeFL‑F by more as K grows: +8.7 at K = 50 and +6.7 at K = 100 for HWA + LA + CSL. But the ranking among our own variants changes with K. At K = 10 the repaired CVAE‑F is best (87.9 against 87.0 for FSG). At K = 50 the variants are within noise of each other (MIX 87.9, FSG 87.7, ours 86.8; ours vs FSG p = 0.47). At K = 100 the Gaussian sufficient-statistics generator leads, 86.1 against 82.7 (p = 0.105, 3 seeds). The reason is structural. FSG's federated estimate is exactly the centralised one at any K, because it aggregates sums. The CVAE's class rows, even with HWA, are fitted by clients that each hold fewer and fewer samples. Which generator to use is therefore a function of the per-client sample size, and the right choice can be read from the aggregated counts the server already has.

On SVHN the ranking holds. Under the long tail, ours is +12.0 points balanced accuracy over GeFL‑F and +6.0 over LA alone. Tail recall nearly doubles (47.8 against 24.8), and the paper's own metric rises by +9.5 (p = 0.045). In the IID setting, our GeFL‑F reaches 75.82, against the paper's 76.26. CSL gains on every seed (+0.78, +0.37, +0.29), reaching 76.30. HWA+LA costs 0.18 points in IID data. The IID split gives clients slightly unequal counts, so HWA is not exactly FedAvg there. The loss is small but consistent across seeds, so we report it.

On CIFAR‑10 under the long tail (partial: 2–3 seeds), GeFL‑F shows the strongest collapse we have measured. The tail/head row ratio is 0.06, and HWA restores it to 1.1–1.3. HWA + LA reaches 40.4 against 33.7 for GeFL‑F and 37.3 for LA alone. Here, unlike SVHN, the Gaussian generator is best, at 43.4: its tail fidelity is 0.47, against 0.25 for the repaired CVAE‑F. On CIFAR‑10 features, a class mean and a pooled covariance estimated exactly from sums beat a CVAE trained on about 25 tail samples. On SVHN, which is multi-modal, the opposite holds. Which generator wins depends on the data. HWA + LA is the choice that is never far behind.

On SVHN the Gaussian generator (FSG) fails. Its class-conditional Gaussian cannot represent SVHN's multi-modal features: tail fidelity is 0.14 against 0.36 for HWA. That failure is why FSG is not our final method, although it was competitive on MNIST.
