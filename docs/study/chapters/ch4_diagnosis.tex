\section{Where GeFL-F loses the rare classes}\label{ch:diagnosis}

The paper finds a candidate loss on each of the first three routes of \cref{sec:s-routes}: the optimiser in Stage II,
the last layer in Stage III, and the synthetic data in Stage III. The fourth route, sample size, is not a loss of
GeFL-F but a constraint on any repair: it decides how the exact readout of \cref{ch:method} has to estimate its
covariance. The evidence for the three losses differs in strength, and the paper says so.

\subsection{The generator: coupled weight decay under Adam}\label{sec:s-decay}

\paragraph{The situation} In one round of Stage II, every client starts from the global generator, takes some local
Adam steps on its own features, and returns its copy; the server averages the copies. Consider the class row $r_9$ of
the running example on client 2, which holds no image of class 9. Client 2 never generates a class-9 feature during
training, so its data loss does not depend on $r_9$ at all. The only force on $r_9$ is the weight decay. With coupled
decay the penalty $\frac\lambda2\|\theta\|^2$ is part of the loss, so the gradient of client 2's loss with respect to
$r_9$ is exactly
\[
g=\lambda r_9 .
\]
With plain SGD this would move $r_9$ by $\eta\lambda r_9$ per step, a tiny amount: with the generator's learning rate
$\eta=10^{-3}$ and decay $\lambda=10^{-3}$, one part in a million of the row per step. Adam behaves very differently.

\paragraph{Why Adam makes the decay large} Adam divides each gradient coordinate by a running estimate of its own
magnitude. With the running means $m$ of the gradient and $v$ of its square (decay rates $\beta_1=0.9$,
$\beta_2=0.999$; these $\beta$s belong to Adam only) and their bias-corrected versions $\hat m=m/(1-\beta_1^t)$ and
$\hat v=v/(1-\beta_2^t)$ after $t$ steps, a step is
\[
\theta\leftarrow\theta-\eta\,\frac{\hat m}{\sqrt{\hat v}+\epsilon},\qquad\epsilon=10^{-8}.
\]
Take, for illustration, one coordinate of $r_9$ equal to $0.5$, so $g=\lambda\cdot0.5=5\times10^{-4}$, and start from
$m=v=0$.
\begin{itemize}
\item Step 1: $m=0.1\,g$ and $v=0.001\,g^2$. The corrections divide by $1-0.9=0.1$ and $1-0.999=0.001$, so $\hat m=g$,
$\hat v=g^2$, and the step is $\eta\,g/(|g|+\epsilon)\approx\eta=10^{-3}$ toward zero.
\item Step 2: the coordinate is now $0.499$, so the gradient is still $g$ to three digits. Then
$m=0.9\cdot0.1g+0.1g=0.19\,g$ and $v=0.999\cdot0.001g^2+0.001g^2=0.001999\,g^2$. The corrections divide by
$1-0.9^2=0.19$ and $1-0.999^2=0.001999$, so again $\hat m=g$, $\hat v=g^2$, and the step is again $\approx\eta$.
\end{itemize}
As long as the gradient keeps its sign and size, every step is one learning rate. Now repeat with a decay 100 times
smaller, $\lambda=10^{-5}$: then $g=5\times10^{-6}$, still much larger than $\epsilon$, and every step is still
$\approx10^{-3}$. The reason is that multiplying every gradient by a number $a>0$ multiplies $\hat m$ by $a$ and
$\sqrt{\hat v}$ by $a$, so their ratio does not change: Adam's step depends on the sign and consistency of the
gradient, not on its size. A client that lacks class 9 therefore moves every coordinate of $r_9$ toward zero by about
one learning rate per step, whatever the decay, 2,000 times faster than SGD would in this example.

\begin{lemma*}[coupled decay under Adam]
On a client without class $c$, the gradient of the loss with coupled L2 regularisation $\lambda$ with respect to $r_c$
is exactly $\lambda r_c$. Since Adam is invariant to a positive rescaling of the gradient sequence up to its $\epsilon$,
each step of such a client moves every coordinate of $r_c$ toward zero by about the learning rate $\eta$, independently
of $\lambda$, as long as $\lambda|r_{c,j}|\gg\epsilon$. With $\lambda=0$ this shrinkage vanishes; with decoupled weight
decay (AdamW) it becomes a factor $1-\eta\lambda$ per step, which is negligible here.
\end{lemma*}

The proof is the two observations above. A client keeps its Adam state between rounds while its parameters are reset
to the global ones, so a single step can be somewhat larger than $\eta$.

\paragraph{Why pooled training and the holders are different} On pooled data, every batch that contains class 9
gives $r_9$ a \emph{data} gradient, much larger than $\lambda r_9$. Adam's $v$ for those coordinates is then dominated
by the data gradients, and a step on which only the decay acts is divided by that large $\sqrt{\hat v}$, so it is
small. This holds as long as class-9 batches come within the memory of $v$, about $1/(1-\beta_2)=1000$ steps. The same
holds on a client that holds class 9. Stage II trains the generator for 5 local epochs per round in batches of 64
(\texttt{local\_train.local\_gen} in \texttt{fedecs\_lt/src}). Client 1 of the running example holds 4 class-9 images
among 913, so between 1 and 4 of its 15 batches per epoch contain the class: a class-9 batch comes at least once every 15 steps, far within Adam's memory, and its decay-only steps stay small. A holder therefore neither shrinks the row much nor
lengthens it: its data gradient fits the row to its few images, which need not make the row longer. The non-holders,
clients 2 and 5, shrink it by a full learning rate on every step.

\paragraph{Averaging turns this into contraction} After the round, the server averages the returned rows. Let
$\Delta_k=\|r_{k,c}\|-\|r_c\|$ be the change of the length of class $c$'s row on client $k$ during the round. Let
$\bar G_c$ be the mean of $\Delta_k$ over the $m_c$ clients that hold the class (the \emph{holders}), $\bar D_c$ the
mean of $-\Delta_k$ over the others (a positive number when they shrink the row), and $p_c=m_c/K$ the share of holders.

\begin{lemma*}[contraction under flat averaging]
Under $r_c^+=\frac1K\sum_k r_{k,c}$, $\|r_c^+\|\le\|r_c\|+p_c\bar G_c-(1-p_c)\bar D_c$, with equality if and only if all
$r_{k,c}$ are non-negative multiples of one another. The row contracts whenever $p_c<\bar D_c/(\bar G_c+\bar D_c)$.
\end{lemma*}

The proof is the triangle inequality: the length of an average is at most the average of the lengths,
$\frac1K\sum_k\|r_{k,c}\|=\|r_c\|+\frac1K\sum_k\Delta_k=\|r_c\|+p_c\bar G_c-(1-p_c)\bar D_c$. The bound is negative when
$p_c\bar G_c<(1-p_c)\bar D_c$, which rearranges to $p_c<\bar D_c/(\bar G_c+\bar D_c)$. The inequality is often strict,
because clients that fit the row to their own few images move it in different directions, and averaging vectors that
point in different directions shortens them further.

The two classes of the running example show the difference. Class 0 is held by all ten clients, so $p_0=1$: there
are no non-holders, the decay term is absent, and the row can only shrink through the triangle inequality. Class 9 is
held by eight clients, $p_9=0.8$. Take, for illustration, $\bar G_9=0.02$ and $\bar D_9=0.05$: the threshold is
$0.05/0.07=0.71$, below $0.8$, and the bound allows a gain of $0.8\cdot0.02-0.2\cdot0.05=0.006$ per round. If the
holders' mean change is zero, which the previous paragraph makes plausible, the threshold is $1$, above $0.8$, and the
row shrinks by at least $0.2\cdot0.05=0.01$ every round, over 200 rounds. \Cref{fig:s-shrink} draws this picture with
made-up numbers.

The paper also gives a predictor of the direction, counting Adam steps:
\[
\kappa_c=\frac{\sum_{k\in H_c}q_k f_{kc}}{\sum_{k\notin H_c}q_k},
\]
where $H_c$ is the set of holders, $q_k$ the number of local steps of client $k$, and $f_{kc}$ the probability that a
batch of client $k$ contains class $c$. The numerator counts the steps on which a holder actually sees the class, the
denominator the steps on which a non-holder decays it; a holder's other steps are neglected, which the previous
paragraph justifies. When $\kappa_c<1$ the row drifts toward zero. The paper is explicit that this is a heuristic, not
a bound: it ignores the momentum Adam carries between rounds, and it was \emph{not} checked against the measured rows.

\paragraph{The remedies} Two changes remove the effect. Decoupled weight decay (AdamW) applies the decay outside
Adam's normalisation, so the shrinkage becomes the negligible factor $1-\eta\lambda$. Or the rows can be averaged over
their holders only, weighted by how much of the class each holds (class-count weighted averaging, CCWA, in
\cref{sec:s-gen}): the non-holders' term $\bar D_c$ then drops out of the lemma.

\paragraph{What was measured} The paper's figure of the class rows (\cref{fig:s-rows}) shows that after Stage II the
rows of the rarest classes are several times shorter than those of the most frequent ones. That is the predicted
effect, although the lengths do not fall monotonically: class 9's row is longer than those of classes 7 and 8.

A second measurement limits what this shows. After Stage I the server trains a classifier, the \emph{referee}, on the
features of its validation images, and asks it to classify 300 generated features of each class. The share it assigns
to the intended class is the generator's \emph{fidelity} for that class. If the decay were what makes rare classes
fail, the rare classes' fidelity would be low and the frequent classes' high. Instead GeFL-F's generated CIFAR-10-LT
features are recognised at \R{main/cifar10/GeFL-F/fidelity_all} on average (chance is 10\%),
\R{main/cifar10/GeFL-F/fidelity_head} on the head classes and \R{main/cifar10/GeFL-F/fidelity_tail} on the tail
classes. On CIFAR-100-LT the mean is \R{main/cifar100/GeFL-F/fidelity_all}, chance level (\cref{tab:s-fidelity}). Besides the
tail classes of \cref{ch:gefl}, the paper reports the \emph{head classes}, the 30\% most frequent, and the
\emph{medium} classes in between. The referee's accuracy on held-out real features was not
logged, so the fidelity values compare generators with each other, not with real data.

\paragraph{How strong the evidence is} The shrinkage is measured and predicted by the lemmas. That it causes the rare
classes' low accuracy is not shown: GeFL-F's generator carries little class information for \emph{any} class, and the
long head-class rows do not make the head classes recognisable. The direct test is GeFL-F with AdamW and nothing else
changed, one of the runs not yet returned (\cref{sec:s-pending}).

\subsection{The last layer: logit corrections cannot add information}\label{sec:s-lastlayer}

\paragraph{The situation} In Stage III, client 2's head is trained on client 2's features only, in the real phase,
with the cross-entropy loss $\ell(f,y)=\LSE(f)-f_y$, where $\LSE(f)=\log\sum_c e^{f_c}$. Its last layer therefore
learns client 2's class proportions: classes it holds often get large biases, and the classes it lacks (class 9 among
them) get pushed down by every step, because cross-entropy lowers the logits of all wrong classes. The natural fix
from long-tail learning is to correct the loss with the client's class frequencies $\hat\pi_k$ (client $k$'s class
counts with one added to each, divided by their total, so that absent classes get a small non-zero frequency). Three
methods of the paper do this by adding a constant vector $a_k$ to the logits during training and not at test time:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{logit adjustment} and \emph{Balanced Softmax}: $a_k=\tau\log\hat\pi_k$, with $\tau=1$;
\item \emph{FedLC}: $a_{kc}=-\tau(n_{kc}+1)^{-1/4}$, which lowers the logits of rare classes during training
(\texttt{fedecs.client\_loss\_adj});
\item \emph{FedRS} instead multiplies the logits of the classes the client lacks by $\alpha=0.5$ during training.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Post-hoc logit adjustment} is the version without retraining: train plainly, then subtract $\tau\log\hat\pi$ from
the logits at test time, which raises the classes the training data made rare.

\paragraph{The statement} Write $\theta$ for the parameters of the head's body and $(W,b)$ for its last layer.

\begin{lemma*}[logit corrections are reparametrisations]
Let a head have a free last-layer bias $b$, trained by SGD without decay on $b$, and let $a_k\in\Rset^C$ be a constant
of client $k$, as in logit adjustment ($a_k=\tau\log\hat\pi_k$), FedLC or Balanced Softmax. Training with the loss
$\ell(f+a_k,y)$ from $(\theta_0,b_0)$ produces exactly the trajectory of training with $\ell(f,y)$ from
$(\theta_0,b_0+a_k)$: every parameter except $b$ is the same at every step. FedRS, which scales the logits of absent
classes by $\alpha\in(0,1]$, is cross-entropy in the rescaled last-layer variables $\alpha w_c,\alpha b_c$ of those
classes, trained with the smaller learning rate $\alpha^2\eta$.
\end{lemma*}

The proof: $f+a_k=We+(b+a_k)$, and moving from $b$ to $b+a_k$ is a translation, which does not change gradients; by
induction over the steps the two trajectories agree. So training with the corrected loss is plain training that
starts from the bias $b_0+a_k$, and leaving the correction out at test time subtracts $a_k$: post-hoc logit adjustment
of a head trained from a shifted starting bias. For FedRS, write $\tilde w_c=\alpha w_c$; the gradient with respect to
$\tilde w_c$ is $1/\alpha$ times the gradient with respect to $w_c$, so a step of $\eta$ on $w_c$ moves $\tilde w_c$ by
$\alpha^2\eta$ times that gradient, while the body sees the same logits.

\paragraph{A three-class example} A client holds 70 images of class A, 30 of class B and none of class C. With add-one
smoothing its frequencies are $(71,31,1)/103$, so $a=\log\hat\pi\approx(-0.37,\,-1.20,\,-4.63)$. At test time the
corrected head adds $+4.63$ to class C's logit relative to plain training, $+3.43$ more than to class B. That raises
class C for \emph{every} input by the same amount. It cannot make class C high for images of class C and low for others,
because the weight vector $w_C$ never received a gradient that points toward class-C features: the client has none.
The correction moves a threshold; it adds no information about the class. The body can still learn a somewhat
different representation, because the shifted logits change the gradients that reach it, but nothing tells it what
class C looks like. The literature calls the degradation of rare classes' weight vectors under imbalance
\emph{minority collapse}; a head trained on one client is its extreme case, because for the classes the client lacks
the imbalance ratio is infinite.

\paragraph{The scope of the lemma} It is exact for a head trained only on real data with one fixed offset, which is
how the paper runs Balanced Softmax, FedLC and FedRS. It does not cover FedECS-LT's heads, whose real phase uses logit
adjustment while their synthetic phase does not: the offset is applied in one of two alternating phases, so it is no
longer a change of starting point. That is why logit adjustment can matter inside FedECS-LT (\cref{sec:s-heads}).

\paragraph{What was measured} First, replacing GeFL-F's trained last layers by the exact readout of
\cref{sec:s-readout}, with nothing else changed, raises its balanced accuracy by \R{main/cifar10/GeFL-F/gain_er} points
on CIFAR-10-LT and \R{main/cifar100/GeFL-F/gain_er} on CIFAR-100-LT. Second, once the last layers of Balanced Softmax,
FedLC and FedRS are replaced by the same readout, the three reach \R{main/cifar10/Balanced Softmax/er_bal},
\R{main/cifar10/FedLC/er_bal} and \R{main/cifar10/FedRS/er_bal} on CIFAR-10-LT, and
\R{main/cifar100/Balanced Softmax/er_bal}, \R{main/cifar100/FedLC/er_bal} and \R{main/cifar100/FedRS/er_bal} on
CIFAR-100-LT. The lemma does not predict this equality, since different offsets give the bodies different gradients;
it is consistent with it: whatever the offsets do to the bodies, they make no difference once the last layer is
replaced.

\paragraph{How strong the evidence is} The lemma is proved, and the readout gain is measured. Together they suggest
that a large part of what GeFL-F loses is in its last layers, and that a last layer that knows the absent classes needs
information from other clients. The partition-invariance lemma of \cref{ch:sums} says where to get it: a last layer
that is a function of sums over all clients equals the one computed on pooled data. That is the paper's \emph{exact
readout}, a classifier computed from summed embedding statistics (\cref{sec:s-readout}).

\subsection{The synthetic data: label-free features penalise the logit spread}\label{sec:s-spread}

\paragraph{The situation} In the synthetic phase, labels are drawn uniformly over the $C$ classes and the generator
makes a feature for each. If the generated feature carries its label, the head learns the class. If it does not, as
the fidelity of \cref{tab:s-fidelity} suggests for GeFL-F, the label is effectively independent of the feature, and
the training target is the uniform distribution.

\begin{lemma*}[label-free synthetic data]
If the synthetic label $y$ is uniform on $\{1,\dots,C\}$ and independent of the generated feature, then for every logit
vector $f$,
\[
\E_y\,\ell(f,y)=\LSE(f)-\bar f=\log C+\KL(u\,\|\,\mathrm{softmax}f)=\log C+\frac1{2C}\sum_c(f_c-\bar f)^2+O(\|f-\bar f\|^3),
\]
where $\bar f$ is the mean logit and $u$ the uniform distribution.
\end{lemma*}

The proof: averaging $\ell(f,y)=\LSE(f)-f_y$ over a uniform $y$ replaces $f_y$ by the mean $\bar f$; the KL form
follows from the definition of $\KL$; and a second-order expansion of $\LSE$ around equal logits gives the quadratic
term. In words: averaged over the random label, the cross-entropy is smallest when all logits are equal, and it grows
with their variance. The paper measures this as the \emph{logit spread}, the mean over images of the standard
deviation $s$ of a head's logits across classes. Since $\frac1C\sum_c(f_c-\bar f)^2=s^2$, the quadratic term is
exactly $s^2/2$: training on label-free features penalises the square of the measured quantity.

A numerical check with $C=10$: equal logits give the loss $\log10=2.303$. The logits $(2,0,\dots,0)$ give
$\LSE=\log(e^2+9)=2.797$ and $\bar f=0.2$, so the loss is $2.597$. Their spread is $s=0.6$, and the quadratic term
alone predicts $2.303+0.6^2/2=2.483$; the rest is the higher-order term. The more confident the head, the larger the
penalty, whatever the class.

\paragraph{What survives the alternation} The synthetic phase is followed by the real phase, which pushes the logits
apart again for the classes the client holds. The paper analyses the alternation in an illustrative model in which
both losses are quadratic (an appendix lemma). There one round is an affine map of the parameters, a contraction
under a stated condition, so the rounds converge to its fixed point. At that point, along every direction of the
parameters that the real loss leaves free, the synthetic loss alone decides. Real heads are nonlinear and trained with
cross-entropy, so this suggests, without proving, what happens to them. For a head trained on one client, the
directions that concern its absent classes are nearly free in this sense, so there the label-free synthetic phase
would decide, and it would flatten exactly the logits the client most needs. Nearly, not exactly, because
cross-entropy on the real data keeps pushing the absent classes' logits down. Logit adjustment weakens this push: with
the offset $\tau\log\hat\pi_k$, an absent class's adjusted logit is already very low, so the real loss is satisfied
without lowering its raw logit further.

\paragraph{What was measured} The paper's figure of the logit spread (\cref{fig:s-spread}) shows, in every Stage-III
round on CIFAR-10-LT, that the synthetic phase reduces the spread of the heads' logits on the clients' real features,
and that it reduces it less for FedECS-LT than for GeFL-F.

\paragraph{How strong the evidence is} The lemma is exact; how much it matters in the pipeline is not settled, for
two reasons. FedECS-LT also differs from GeFL-F in its number of synthetic epochs, its soft targets and logit
adjustment (all in \cref{sec:s-heads}), so the comparison does not isolate the labels. And FedDF, which trains on
GeFL-F's own label-free generator, loses \emph{less} spread than FedECS-LT, so the size of the spread change is not
determined by whether the generated features carry their label.
