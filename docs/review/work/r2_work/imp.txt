 Class-Balanced GeFL‑F Derivations
 What can be proven, what can be compared to the original paper, and how each design decision is defended 
 A hypothesis becomes defensible when its formula follows from a stated model of the problem, its
 comparisons are controlled, and its predictions can fail. This document applies that standard to the
 two proposed mechanisms. One survives intact and gains an exact proof. The other survives only in a
 reformulated version, and only with a hard ceiling that changes what the paper should claim.

 00 Verdict 
 Read this first. Everything after it is the supporting argument. 
 Defensible as proposed Mechanism A 
 Flat averaging provably shrinks every class's conditioning update by the fraction of clients holding that class. This is an exact identity, verified numerically. Mechanism A's weighting is the minimum-variance estimator under a stated noise model, and flat averaging is optimal only in the IID-balanced regime the paper tested. 
 Defensible only reformulated Mechanism B 
 No choice of sampling distribution can balance the header. The synthetic phase is one-sixth of training exposure, so the best possible p(y) still leaves a 7.5× head-to-tail gap. Its correct form is derived here (client-local water-filling), but it cannot be the headline. 
 What the math adds A correction after the real phase 
 The dominant imbalance enters in the real-data phase, which runs last and is five times longer. Logit adjustment is the provably correct correction for that term (Fisher-consistent for balanced error). It should become a component of the method, not only a baseline. 
 Correction to an earlier claim 
 An earlier version of the project review stated that the “support floor” variant
 hurt results and presented it as a negative finding. That was wrong , and
 the review and paper skeleton have since been corrected. The floor switches on only when a
 class has fewer than 20 samples across a round's clients. With all 10 clients participating, the
 rarest CIFAR‑10‑LT class has 25, so the floor never activated. The “floor” runs
 were exact repeats of their non-floor configurations, and any differences between them are run-to-run
 noise. Section 3.4 turns this accident into something useful.
 01 How this field defends design decisions 
 The papers this work will be judged against each justify themselves in a recognisable way. Matching that standard is the bar. 
 Four defence patterns, where they come from, and what each demands of this project 
 Pattern Exemplar How they did it What it demands of us 
 Derive the formula from a stated model Cui et al., CVPR 2019 
 The effective number (1−β n )/(1−β) is not guessed. It falls out of modelling sampling as random covering, where each new sample overlaps old ones with probability β. 
 Mechanism A's weights must follow from an explicit noise model (§4). Mechanism B's sampling rule must solve a stated objective (§5). 
 Prove consistency for the reported metric Menon et al., ICLR 2021 
 Logit adjustment is shown to be Fisher-consistent for the balanced error , the very metric long-tail papers report. 
 Our headline metric is bucketed / balanced accuracy, so we should prefer corrections with this guarantee (§6). 
 Diagnose before proposing Shang et al. (CReFF), IJCAI 2022 
 Before introducing the method, they re-train each component of a FedAvg model separately on balanced data and show the classifier alone recovers most of the gap. 
 We need the analogous diagnostic: a measurement showing where GeFL‑F loses the tail. Results 1 and 3 supply it analytically; the experiments in §8 confirm it. 
 Controlled, protocol-matched comparison CReFF; ablation-design literature 
 “We implement all compared FL methods with the same model for a fair comparison.” Change one factor at a time, fix seeds, report variance. 
 Baseline GeFL‑F must be re-run in our protocol, never quoted from the paper. Each mechanism must be ablated alone and in combination (§8). 
 A fifth pattern recurs in the reviewer-facing literature: make a prediction that could fail. 
 A method that merely improves a number is weaker than one whose theory predicts how the
 improvement should scale , followed by a test of that prediction. Result 1 gives exactly such a
 prediction, and it happens to fall on the scalability axis the GeFL‑F paper itself studies.
 02 Comparability with the original paper 
 The worry is legitimate: our implementation differs from the paper's. Some differences only shift absolute numbers; others would invalidate a comparison. They need to be separated. 
 2.1 What the paper actually measured 
 Reading the reference implementation, not just the paper text, shows the protocol behind every reported GeFL‑F number: 
 Partition: dict_iid , an IID split of a balanced dataset. The paper never evaluates long-tailed or Dirichlet-skewed data. 
 Feature extractor: Conv(in→3) + BN + ReLU + MaxPool on 32×32 input, giving a 3×16×16 feature (768 values). 
 Headers: ten CNNs from the reference targetNetModels/cnn.py . 
 Reported metric: evaluate_models keeps the best accuracy each architecture reaches over all rounds, and the paper averages those maxima. It is a best-over-rounds number, not a final-round one. 
 Consequence 
 There is no long-tailed number in the paper to compare against, so none of our long-tail results can
 be set beside the paper's table. This is not a weakness to hide; it is the gap the project
 exists to fill. The defensible protocol has three tiers. Tier 1 shows our
 GeFL‑F is GeFL‑F by reproducing one cell of the paper under its own protocol.
 Tier 2 compares baseline and proposed inside our long-tail protocol, with identical
 everything else. Tier 3 tests the mechanisms' predictions directly. This is the same
 structure CReFF uses.
 2.2 The conditioning pathway is the same object in both implementations 
 The most important comparability question is whether replacing the reference's one-hot conditioning
 with an nn.Embedding changed the method. The reference decoder concatenates a one-hot
 label to the latent and applies a linear layer. Split that layer's weight into the columns acting on
 z and the columns acting on the one-hot:
 W [ z ; e c  ]  =  W z z  +  W y e c 
  =  W z z  +  w c 
      w c = column c of W y 
 (1) 
 A one-hot input to a linear layer is an embedding lookup: column c of
 W y is a learned per-class vector, and it receives gradient only
 from samples of class c , because the input coordinate is zero otherwise.
 Mechanism A is therefore well-defined on the unmodified reference architecture : its
 conditioning parameters are those columns. Our embedding is a rank-limited factorisation of the same
 object ( W e E[c] , with a 16-dimensional embedding). This turns
 “we changed the architecture to suit our method” into “our method applies to the
 original architecture; we use an equivalent factorised parameterisation.” We still control for it
 with an ablation.
 One honest asymmetry: the reference encoder conditions on the label as a single constant
 channel holding the integer class value, an ordinal encoding with no per-class parameters. Mechanism A
 has nothing to act on there. This does not matter for generation, which uses only the decoder.

 03 Result 1: flat averaging damps every class's conditioning update 
 The formal statement of what “class-blind aggregation” costs. It is exact, not an approximation. 
 Theorem 1 — damping identity exact · verified numerically 
 Let W r be the conditioning parameters of class r ,
 meaning an embedding row or, equivalently, a one-hot column (Eq. 1). Let
 H r be the set of participating clients holding at least one
 sample of class r , with m r = |H r | 
 out of K . If local training uses SGD or Adam without weight decay ,
 then flat averaging produces
 W r t+1  =  W r t  + 
 m r K 
 · Δ̄ r ,
 Δ̄ r = 1 m r Σ k∈H r Δ k,r 
 (2) 
 so every class's update is scaled by the fraction of clients that hold it. Mechanism A, with weights a k summing to 1 over H r , gives W r t+1 = W r t + Σ k∈H r a k Δ k,r , with step factor 1. 
 Proof. The loss gradient with respect to W r 
 sums only over samples labelled r . For a client
 k ∉ H r that sum is empty at every local step, so the gradient
 on W r is identically zero. Under SGD the update is zero. Under
 Adam the first and second moment estimates stay exactly zero, so the update
 m̂/(√v̂ + ε) = 0 . Hence
 W k,r = W r t for every non-holder. Then
 (1/K)Σ k W k,r = W r t + (1/K)Σ k∈H r Δ k,r ,
 which is Eq. 2. ∎

 3.3 The prediction this gives — and why it is the strongest card 
 At 10 clients the tail damping is moderate (0.71), so Theorem 1 predicts Mechanism A's benefit there is
 modest. It also predicts the benefit should grow as clients increase , because flat
 averaging degrades from 0.71 to 0.33 on the tail while Mechanism A stays at 1. This is a falsifiable
 prediction on precisely the axis the GeFL‑F paper uses to argue its own value (its Figure 4 sweeps
 10, 50 and 100 clients). If the measured gain from Mechanism A rises with K , the
 theory is confirmed on the paper's home ground. If it does not, something other than damping limits the
 tail, and we will have learned that too.

 04 Result 2: why Mechanism A uses these particular weights 
 Theorem 1 says that non-holders should be excluded. This says how the holders should be weighted, from a stated model. 
 Proposition 2 — Mechanism A is inverse-variance weighting under a stated noise model 
 Model each holder's update as an unbiased, independent, noisy estimate of a common ideal update,
 Δ k,r = δ r + ξ k , with
 Var(ξ k ) = σ 2 / I(n k,r ) , where
 I(n) is the information carried by n samples. The
 minimum-variance unbiased linear combination is
 a k  =  I(n k,r ) Σ j I(n j,r ) 
 I(n) = n  (iid samples)   or   I(n) = E(n) = 1−β n 1−β  (Cui's covering model)
 (3) 
 Sketch. Minimise Var(Σa k Δ k ) = σ 2 Σa k 2 /I k 
 subject to Σa k = 1 (unbiasedness). The Lagrangian gives
 a k ∝ I k , the classical inverse-variance estimator.
 A client with n k,r = 0 carries no information, so it gets weight 0,
 consistent with Theorem 1. ∎
 This answers the “why this direction?” question from first principles: Cui et al. use
 1/E(n) to reweight a loss , while we use E(n) 
 to weight independent estimates . The two answer different questions, and Eq. 3 shows which
 applies here.
 It also gives the most persuasive sentence available to the paper. The flat average is the
 minimum-variance estimator if and only if every holder has the same count , which is
 exactly the IID-balanced regime in which GeFL‑F was evaluated. Class-blind aggregation costs
 nothing in the paper's own setting and grows costly away from it. The authors did not make a mistake;
 their setting simply never exposed it.

 The ratio (Σ1/n k )(Σn k )/m 2 is at
 least 1 by Cauchy–Schwarz, with equality only when all counts are equal. Note an honest
 subtlety: the variance penalty is largest for head classes , where counts are most uneven across
 clients. The tail benefits mainly through Theorem 1's damping. The two results act on different classes,
 which is worth saying explicitly.
 Whether to use n or E(n) barely matters here 
 At β = 0.999 , E(n) ≈ n for small
 counts ( E(25) = 24.7 ) and saturates by only about 21% at
 n = 500 . The defensible core of Mechanism A is therefore “weight by
 evidence, give zero to clients with none.” Effective number is a refinement. A clean ablation is
 I(n) = n against I(n) = E(n) : if they tie, say so.
 05 Result 3: no sampling distribution can balance the header 
 Mechanism B reshapes p(y). This section bounds how much that can ever achieve. 
 5.1 An exposure model 
 In stage iii each round runs T s = 1 synthetic epoch and
 T r = 5 real epochs, with the synthetic phase sized to one real
 epoch's batch count. Let ε c be the fraction of synthetic
 class- c samples that do not resemble class c . To first
 order, the useful per-class training exposure per round, in units of one epoch, is
 U c  =  T s  (1 − ε c ) p(c)  +  T r  π(c),
      noise mass  =  T s  Σ c  ε c  p(c)
 (4) 
 The balanced error weights every class equally, so the target is
 U c equal across classes. Aggregation averages client updates, which
 to first order sums their gradients, so the global π governs the aggregated
 header.
 Proposition 3 — the sampling ceiling first-order model 
 Over all p on the simplex, the most balanced achievable exposure, meaning
 the largest possible worst-class U c , is attained by water-filling:
 p * (c)  =  [ λ − T r  π(c) ] + T s  (1 − ε c ) ,
     λ chosen so that Σ c p * (c) = 1
 (5) 
 Because the synthetic phase holds only T s /(T s +T r ) = 1/6 
 of total exposure, the residual imbalance after the optimal p * is bounded away from 1.
 Sketch. Maximising min c U c with
 U c affine in p under a budget constraint is
 the standard water-filling problem: raise every class below level λ up
 to λ , starting from the lowest, until the budget is spent. Classes with
 T r π(c) ≥ λ receive no synthetic samples. ∎

 5.2 What this means for Mechanism B 
 It cannot be the headline. Its improvement is capped by construction. The gate sweep agrees: B alone gave +3.3 pp tail, a real but bounded effect. 
 Its correct form differs from the current code. The header is trained locally, so the right π in Eq. 5 is the client-local distribution π k , not the global one the current sampler uses. Per-client head-to-tail ratios on the real partition range from 1.0× to 41×, so one global distribution is wrong for almost every client. 
 The fidelity gate is now derived, not heuristic. Eq. 5 multiplies by 1/(1−ε c ) , which increases sampling of badly rendered classes and with it the noise mass. Bounding the noise, Σε c p(c) ≤ η , or excluding classes with ε c > ε max , is what a principled fidelity gate does. Call the derived version Mechanism B′: gated, client-local water-filling. 
 06 Result 4: the correction the mathematics points to 
 If the dominant imbalance enters in the real phase, the fix has to act after it. 
 Proposition 3 locates the problem: five-sixths of the header's exposure comes from the real-data phase,
 which follows the natural long-tailed π and runs last . Sequential
 training then pulls the header back toward the real-data prior, so the first-order model, if anything,
 overstates how much the synthetic phase can achieve. Any correction placed before the real phase
 is diluted by it.
 Two corrections act after it, and both have a pedigree: 
 Post-real-phase corrections 
 Correction Guarantee Cost Role 
 Logit adjustment (Menon et al.): predict with arg max c f c (x) − τ log π(c) 
 Fisher-consistent for balanced error, our reported metric none at training; one hyperparameter 
 Directly corrects the real-phase prior that Proposition 3 identifies as dominant. 
 Balanced classifier re-training (decoupling; CReFF): after stage iii, re-fit only the final layer on synthetic features drawn equally per class 
 Empirical, but CReFF's own diagnostic shows the classifier holds most of the gap in federated long-tail learning a short extra stage 
 The feature generator makes a perfectly balanced set available at every client, which ordinary FL cannot do. 
 This turns a weakness into structure. Each component of the method maps to one term in the analysis:
 Mechanism A removes the damping in the generator's conditioning (Theorem 1);
 Mechanism B′ spends the synthetic budget optimally within its ceiling
 (Proposition 3); logit adjustment corrects the real-phase prior that no sampling policy
 can reach. A reviewer can see why each component exists and what it is responsible for.
 The risk this creates, stated plainly 
 Logit adjustment is cheap and well established. If it alone matches A + B′ + logit adjustment,
 the generator-side contribution is weak. That outcome would still be publishable: it would show that
 GeFL‑F's tail failure is a decision-boundary problem rather than a generative one. But the
 paper's framing would change. The factorial design in §8 is built to detect this case.
 07 Defects the analysis uncovered 
 7.1  DDPM‑F weight decay erases tail classes 
 Our DDPM‑F uses torch.optim.Adam(weight_decay=1e-3) . Plain Adam adds weight decay to
 the gradient and then normalises the result, so a row whose only gradient is the decay term is still
 pushed toward zero at the full learning rate. The test in §3.1 shows absent-class rows moving
 exactly as fast as held rows (1.2e‑3). Every client that has never seen a class actively shrinks
 that class's conditioning row, and flat averaging then mixes those shrunk rows in. The reference
 implementation uses no weight decay for DDPM. Fix: remove it, or use
 AdamW and exclude embedding parameters, which is standard practice. This must be done
 before any DDPM‑F result is reported.
 7.2  Feature dimensionality 
 Already documented in the technical reference: fe_channels = 32 makes the feature 2.67×
 larger than the image, where the paper's 3 channels make it 4× smaller. This deviation changes
 GeFL‑F itself and must be reverted for headline results.
 7.3  Mechanism B uses the global distribution 
 The current FidelityGatedSampler blends toward inverse global frequency. Section 5.2
 shows the derivable optimum depends on each client's local distribution. This is not a crash,
 but the implementation does not match the version that can be justified.

 09 What the derivations assume 
 Stating these is part of the defence. A reviewer will find them anyway. 
 Theorem 1 needs local optimisers without weight decay and conditioning parameters that are not tied to other parameters. Both hold for CVAE‑F and DCGAN‑F as implemented. It is exact about the update , not about final accuracy; the link to accuracy is an empirical claim (C2). 
 Proposition 2 assumes holder updates are unbiased and independent. Client drift, where local trunks move in different directions, introduces bias that inverse-variance weighting does not correct. The update of row r also interacts with the trunk, which the model ignores. 
 Proposition 3 is a first-order exposure model. It ignores phase ordering (which makes the synthetic phase weaker still, so the ceiling is optimistic for Mechanism B) and assumes one synthetic sample is worth one real one. A diverse synthetic sample could be worth more, which would raise the ceiling but not remove it. 
 ε c is not directly observable. The header-confidence signal currently used to estimate it is partly circular (the header scores samples it trains on). A held-out estimate, such as reconstruction error on unseen local data or a frozen referee classifier, is the defensible measurement.
